# Customization And Rulemaking

## 1. Why customization matters

Brehon Consensus is not intended to impose one rigid code on every community.

Its design assumes:

- local communities will have different norms,
- different severities will call for different thresholds,
- federation relationships will vary,
- communities may want different balances between openness, caution, and restoration.

The system therefore separates:

- stable constitutional principles,
- configurable policy values,
- community-authored rule texts,
- and local federation choices.

## 2. Layers of customization

### 2.1 Constitutional layer

These are the platform-wide commitments least open to local alteration.

Examples:

- no token-based governance,
- no on-chain voting,
- no single reputation score,
- governance decisions must be procedurally logged,
- remote sanctions are not automatically binding merely because they were received.

These are not ordinary per-community toggles. They are part of the system's constitutional identity.

### 2.2 Policy layer

These are adjustable numeric or rule-like settings.

Examples include:

- jury size,
- quorum,
- severity thresholds,
- appeal windows,
- sponsorship cooldowns,
- endorsement limits,
- reputation thresholds,
- sanction durations,
- federation trust defaults,
- grace periods for sponsor liability,
- onboarding gate strategy.

These settings are intended to be configurable and auditable.

### 2.3 Community rule-text layer

Each community may define its own:

- rules,
- interpretations,
- governance text,
- constitutional amendments,
- local case categories,
- and public statements of principle.

This is important because a Brehon-inspired network should preserve local normative life rather than replacing it with a universal administrative manual.

### 2.4 Federation policy layer

Each instance may express judgments about other instances through trust states such as:

- allow,
- limit,
- quarantine,
- block.

Those are governance choices, not purely technical switches.

## 3. Versioning and legal intelligibility

Customization is only legitimate if rules remain intelligible over time.

Therefore the system is intended to support:

- rule-set versioning,
- preservation of the rule version in force when a case was decided,
- traceable policy changes,
- public logging of major governance-setting changes,
- external review of constitutional changes.

This matters because a fair system should not judge a person under one rule and later quietly rewrite the operative rule text.

## 4. What can be customized

The following categories are especially likely to vary between communities or instances.

### 4.1 Onboarding model

Examples:

- strict sponsorship-first,
- age-based progression,
- hybrid age-or-surety approach,
- temporary open enrollment,
- emergency lockdown.

### 4.2 Jury mechanics

Examples:

- jury size,
- maximum simultaneous assignments,
- response windows,
- appeal panel size,
- diversity constraints,
- severity thresholds.

### 4.3 Reputation thresholds

Examples:

- when a member becomes sponsor-eligible,
- when a report gains extra weight,
- when a juror becomes eligible,
- how quickly positive standing decays,
- whether and how negative standing can recover.

### 4.4 Sponsor-liability rules

Examples:

- how liability is split,
- whether founder or high-standing multipliers apply,
- whether a floor prevents going below zero,
- how much grace period exists before sponsor liability crystallizes.

### 4.5 Sanction policy

Examples:

- which sanction types exist locally,
- default durations,
- whether restorative responses are preferred at certain severities,
- whether some sanctions require a stronger threshold than others.

### 4.6 Federation posture

Examples:

- default trust state for new peers,
- whether some peer classes are more trusted,
- how remote signals are surfaced to reviewers,
- what evidence is required before changing a peer's trust state.

## 5. What should not be casually customized

For expert review, this is especially important.

Some matters are likely to be too foundational or too dangerous to treat as casual local preferences.

Examples:

- automatic obedience to remote sanctions,
- elimination of appeals,
- removal of logging and transparency,
- unrestricted administrator override,
- public exposure of reporter identity,
- replacement of multi-dimensional standing with a single score,
- replacement of procedural legitimacy with simple administrator fiat.

A community may adapt many parameters, but some changes would effectively abandon the platform's legal philosophy.

## 6. Governance of customization itself

The system is intended to require formal process for important settings changes.

That means:

- some settings may require step-up authentication,
- some may require multiple approvers,
- some may require delay before taking effect,
- significant changes should be logged publicly or at least in the governance log,
- communities should be able to distinguish between ordinary moderation and constitutional revision.

## 7. Customization and expert review questions

The expert-review database should be able to answer questions such as:

- Which features are globally fixed and which are locally tunable?
- Can a community redefine sanction categories?
- Can a community change jury thresholds?
- Can sponsorship be disabled temporarily?
- How are rule-set changes recorded?
- Does an old case remain attached to the rule version under which it was decided?
- Can a local instance weaken federation safeguards?
- Which settings need multi-party approval?

## 8. Recommended interpretation

The safest way to read the customization model is this:

the platform is **pluralist in policy**, but **not relativist in core safeguards**.

In other words:

- different communities may govern differently,
- but the platform still keeps certain constitutional commitments about transparency, due process, anti-capture, and bounded power.
