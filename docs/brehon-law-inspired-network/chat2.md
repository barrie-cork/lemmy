> **Justice was not enforced by a state, but by social trust, reputation, and mutual obligation**

# 🧩 1. Honor price → Reputation as stake

In Brehon law:

* Every person had an **“honor price”** (status value)
* If they broke rules → they lost it
* Higher-status people had more to lose

### 💡 Platform equivalent

Instead of a score, implement:

### **Reputation as “stake at risk”**

* Users accumulate **social capital**
* When they:

  * make false reports
  * act maliciously
    → they **lose reputation weight**

And crucially:

* High-trust users suffer **bigger consequences when wrong**

👉 Prevents elite abuse

---

# ⚖️ 2. Sureties → Sponsorship / vouching system

In Brehon law:

* People were backed by **sureties** (guarantors)
* If you misbehaved → your guarantor was affected

### 💡 Platform equivalent

### **Web-of-trust onboarding**

* New users must be:

  * invited
  * or endorsed by existing members

If a user is banned:

* their sponsors take a **small reputation hit**

👉 This creates:

* accountability chains
* resistance to fake accounts
* organic trust networks

---

# 👥 3. Kin groups → Community clusters

Irish society was structured around:

* kin groups (fine)
* layered loyalties

### 💡 Platform equivalent

### **Nested communities / trust circles**

* Users belong to:

  * local community (primary trust)
  * optional alliances (secondary trust)

Moderation decisions:

* start local
* escalate to wider network only if needed

👉 Prevents global mob dynamics

---

# 🧑‍⚖️ 4. Brehons → Expert jurors (not rulers)

Brehons:

* didn’t enforce laws
* interpreted them
* were respected for expertise

### 💡 Platform equivalent

### **Weighted juries (but not elites)**

* Random jury still exists
* But:

  * experienced users get **slightly higher influence**
  * not absolute authority

And:

* past jurors build a **track record**

👉 Expertise without centralization

# 💰 5. Restitution over punishment

Brehon law focused on:

* compensation, not punishment

### 💡 Platform equivalent

Instead of only:

* bans

### **Restorative actions**

* apology requirement
* content correction
* temporary visibility limits
* contribution to community (e.g. moderation duty)

👉 Keeps people inside the system instead of ejecting them immediately

Brehon law was:

Yes. For a system like this, I would design security in **two separate layers**: security for the **service itself** and security for the **governance process**. If you only protect the app, attackers can still manipulate moderation or jury outcomes; if you only protect governance, they can still take the platform offline. OWASP explicitly recommends threat modeling early, and CISA/OWASP both emphasize logging, least privilege, and secure design as foundational controls. ([OWASP Cheat Sheet Series][1])

## 1. Protect privileged actions much more heavily than normal posting

The single biggest improvement is to require **phishing-resistant MFA** for stewards, federation admins, jury members handling serious cases, and anyone who can change trust policy or node relationships. NIST’s current guidance says phishing resistance requires cryptographic authentication rather than codes manually typed into a session, and CISA recommends MFA for privileged and internet-accessible accounts. Also add short session lifetimes, idle timeouts, and step-up reauthentication before sensitive actions like quorum changes, sanctions, appeal reversals, or remote-node quarantine. ([NIST Pages][2])

## 2. Separate the governance plane from the social/content plane

I would not let the same code path that serves posts and comments directly finalize sanctions or governance changes. Instead, make governance decisions flow through a separate service or at least a separate permission boundary with stricter authz, narrower network exposure, and a signed audit trail. That recommendation follows OWASP’s guidance that access control should be policy-driven and enforced per resource/action, and that audit trails for sensitive transactions should have integrity controls to prevent tampering or deletion. ([OWASP Cheat Sheet Series][3])

## 3. Treat governance events like high-integrity records

For jury assignments, verdicts, sanctions, appeals, and federation trust changes, use an **append-only event log** with cryptographic signing. OWASP recommends protecting logs from unauthorized modification and deletion, and specifically calls out integrity controls such as append-only storage. Sigstore’s Rekor is a good design inspiration here because it uses an immutable, append-only transparency log that can be publicly audited. In practice, that means every governance event should be hashed, signed, timestamped, and independently verifiable later. ([OWASP Cheat Sheet Series][4])

## 4. Make high-risk governance actions require quorum, not one actor

For actions like banning a trusted member, quarantining a remote node, changing jury-selection policy, or modifying community constitutions, require **multi-party approval** and delay final execution briefly so it can be reviewed. This is partly a security control and partly an anti-coercion control. The underlying principle is least privilege and deny-by-default: no single compromised admin account should be able to irreversibly alter governance. That aligns with OWASP’s broken access control guidance and CISA’s least-privilege recommendations. ([owasp.org][5])

## 5. Harden the federation boundary

Because a federated system accepts remote content and signals, the federation layer should be treated as hostile input. Require HTTPS everywhere for API traffic, verify signatures on federated messages, strictly validate remote objects, rate-limit inbox processing, and isolate media fetching from the main app network path. OWASP’s REST guidance requires HTTPS for secure services and recommends rate limiting; OWASP also treats SSRF as a serious design concern when servers fetch attacker-controlled URLs. ([OWASP Cheat Sheet Series][6])

## 6. Build for DDoS and abuse at the edge

A movement platform is an obvious DDoS target, so front it with a CDN or reverse proxy that can absorb volumetric traffic, enable aggressive rate limiting on login, reporting, voting, and federation endpoints, and keep a response playbook ready. CISA’s DDoS guidance stresses preparation, mitigation, and rapid recovery, and OWASP’s guidance repeatedly points to rate limiting as a core defensive control for authentication and APIs. ([CISA][7])

## 7. Reduce the value of compromised credentials

Even with MFA, assume some accounts will be taken over. Limit what any one account can do, especially new or low-trust accounts. Add anomaly detection for impossible travel, sudden trust-graph changes, mass reporting, mass endorsements, and policy edits outside normal patterns. CISA recommends comprehensive event logging and threat detection, and OWASP recommends testing abuse cases discovered during threat modeling rather than only normal user flows. ([CISA][8])

## 8. Protect secrets and signing keys properly

Do not hardcode secrets in app config or CI. Use a secrets manager, rotate secrets, separate duties, and keep governance-signing keys away from the main app runtime where possible. OWASP’s secrets-management guidance is clear that secrets should be centrally managed, provisioned, audited, and rotated. For software artifacts, sign builds and verify them before deployment; Sigstore’s Cosign is designed for signing and verifying container images and related artifacts, and CISA treats SBOMs as a building block of software supply-chain security. ([OWASP Cheat Sheet Series][9])

## 9. Make compromise recoverable

Plan for restoration before launch. Keep **encrypted, offline, immutable backups** of the database, evidence store, and governance log, and test restoration regularly. CISA repeatedly recommends offline and immutable backups because attackers often try to destroy or encrypt accessible backups during compromise. ([CISA][10])

## 10. Test the system as a governance system, not just a web app

Run threat modeling and abuse-case reviews specifically for scenarios like brigading, juror capture, sponsor farming, fake federated attestations, malicious appeals, and “admin account compromise during a contentious case.” OWASP recommends threat modeling as part of design, and its secure product design guidance recommends converting discovered threats into concrete tests across tiers. ([OWASP Cheat Sheet Series][1])

## What I would implement first

If I had to prioritize, I’d start with this stack:

1. **Phishing-resistant MFA + step-up auth** for all privileged governance actions. ([NIST Pages][2])
2. **Strict authorization and least privilege** for every governance endpoint and object. ([OWASP Cheat Sheet Series][3])
3. **Append-only, signed governance log** for decisions, sanctions, appeals, and federation trust changes. ([owasp.org][11])
4. **Rate limiting and DDoS protection** at the edge and on sensitive APIs. ([CISA][7])
5. **Immutable offline backups** with restore drills. ([CISA][10])
6. **Signed builds, dependency visibility, and secret management** in CI/CD. ([Sigstore][12])

The core idea is simple: **assume attackers will eventually get in somewhere, then make it hard for them to turn that foothold into silent governance capture**. The best defenses are strong auth for privileged users, narrow permissions, tamper-evident decision logs, resilient infrastructure, and recovery paths that preserve trust after an incident. ([owasp.org][11])

# 🧠 What your paper is saying (in plain English)

The paper you linked highlights the core strengths of blockchain voting:

# ⚠️ But here’s the critical reality

Blockchain **solves integrity of data**, but it does NOT solve:

* Identity (who is voting?)
* Sybil attacks (fake accounts)
* Social manipulation
* Jury capture / brigading
* Governance fairness

In fact, research on DAO governance shows:

> small groups often dominate voting power
> (e.g. a handful of users controlling majority votes) ([arxiv.org][3])

👉 This is a **huge warning** for your system.

You’re building:

> federated + reputation + jury-based governance

So blockchain should be used **surgically**, not everywhere.
