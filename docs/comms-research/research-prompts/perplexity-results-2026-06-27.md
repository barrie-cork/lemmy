## 1. Analogy and Concrete-First Explanation

### 1.1 Analogical Transfer: Why Analogies Work

The most robust theoretical account of why analogies aid comprehension is **Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory**, first published in *Cognitive Science* in 1983. Gentner argues that when we understand an analogy, we are not merely mapping surface features (shape, colour, category) from a familiar *base* domain onto an unfamiliar *target* domain — we are mapping **relational structure**: the system of relationships and causal rules that governs the base. The richer and more systematic the relational overlap, the stronger the analogical transfer. A good analogy teaches you *why* something works, not just *what* it resembles.

Gentner's key insight is the **systematicity principle**: people prefer analogies that map coherent, higher-order relational systems (e.g. "the electron orbits the nucleus *because* of electrostatic attraction, just as the planet orbits the sun *because* of gravitational attraction") over analogies that match only isolated object attributes. This is why superficially similar comparisons ("a virus is a bit like a computer bug") are weaker pedagogically than structurally deep ones. The core paper is cited thousands of times across cognitive science and education research. ([Gentner, 1983, *Cognitive Science* 7: 155–170 — see discussion in Gentner et al.'s subsequent case study](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15327809jls0601_2))

**Practical rules derived from structure-mapping theory:**
- Choose a base domain the audience already understands deeply and uses habitually.
- Map the *rules and relationships* of the base (not just its surface appearance) onto the target concept.
- Make the mapping explicit: state aloud which features correspond and, critically, which do *not* (the "limiting conditions" of the analogy).
- Prefer "deep" analogies — those in which the structural correspondence covers multiple levels — over single-attribute comparisons.

### 1.2 Gick & Holyoak's Radiation Problem Studies (1980, 1983)

Two landmark experiments by **Michael Gick and Keith Holyoak** at the University of Michigan provide the strongest empirical evidence that analogies must be explicitly flagged to be effective, and that multiple exemplars greatly improve spontaneous transfer.

**The radiation problem** (Duncker, 1945): A patient has an inoperable stomach tumour. Rays of sufficient intensity will destroy it, but will also destroy the surrounding healthy tissue. How do you use the rays to destroy the tumour without damaging healthy tissue? The convergence solution (fire multiple low-intensity rays from different angles so they converge at the tumour) is rarely generated without a hint.

**Gick & Holyoak (1980)** gave participants an analogous *military story* (a general who must capture a fortress; his large army would destroy roads and villages if it marched en masse, so he splits his forces and converges them simultaneously). When participants had read the military story before attempting the radiation problem, only **30% spontaneously used the analogy**; when they were told explicitly that the story might help, success rose to **76%**. Without any story, the spontaneous solution rate was about 10%. ([Gick & Holyoak (1980), *Cognitive Psychology* 12: 306–355; DOI 10.1016/0010-0285(80)90013-4](https://linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/0010028580900134))

**Gick & Holyoak (1983)** showed that providing *two* analogous stories (rather than one) dramatically increased spontaneous transfer — participants who read two structurally similar stories were far more likely to abstract the underlying convergence schema and apply it without a hint. This is because exposure to multiple examples forces the mind to extract the *relational skeleton* rather than encoding any single surface story. ([Gick & Holyoak (1983), *Cognitive Psychology* 15: 1–38; DOI 10.1016/0010-0285(83)90002-6](https://linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/0010028583900026))

**Practical rules for civic communications:**
1. **Never assume the analogy will transfer silently.** Always name it explicitly: "This is just like [the familiar situation] — the same logic applies here."
2. **Use two or more analogies from different surface domains** that share the same structural logic, to help audiences abstract the underlying principle.
3. **State the limits of the analogy** — where it breaks down — or the audience will over-generalise.

### 1.3 Dual Coding Theory and the Concreteness Effect

**Allan Paivio** (University of Western Ontario) introduced dual coding theory in 1971 and elaborated it substantially in his 1986 book *Mental Representations*. The core claim is that the human mind operates with **two functionally independent but interconnected representational systems**: a *verbal system* (which encodes language in sequential, symbolic form) and a *non-verbal imagery system* (which encodes analogue, sensory representations of objects, scenes, and events). Any stimulus can be encoded in one system, the other, or both. The memory advantage of a dually-coded item is profound: it has *two independent retrieval pathways*, either of which can reinstate the memory. ([Paivio (1986), *Mental Representations*, Oxford University Press; dual coding theory overview at Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2016/entries/mental-imagery/theories-memory.html))

The most direct implication is the **concreteness effect**: concrete words (e.g. *bicycle*, *river*, *protest*) are consistently recalled better than abstract words (e.g. *justice*, *governance*, *consensus*), because concrete words spontaneously activate imagery representations in addition to their verbal encodings, giving them dual-coded storage. The concreteness effect is described as "one of the most robustly replicated findings in verbal-memory research" and is rated as having **strong supporting evidence** in systematic reviews of dual coding predictions. ([Memletics evidence synthesis for dual coding](https://www.memletics.com/foundations-dual-coding-references))

Key empirical support from cognitive neuroscience: ERP and EEG studies confirm that concrete words elicit neural signatures consistent with activation of the non-verbal/imagery system, providing converging evidence for dual coding's two-system architecture. ([Welcome et al. (2011), *Experimental Brain Research* 209: 481–493; DOI 10.1007/s00221-011-2734-8](http://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00221-011-2734-8))

The **picture superiority effect** is a related finding: people remember pictures far better than words, and words accompanied by pictures better than words alone. Paivio's foundational experiment showed that participants presented with a rapid sequence of pictures recalled them better in free recall than a matched sequence of words — consistent with dual coding's prediction that pictures receive automatic verbal labelling and thus dual encoding.

**Practical rule:** Every abstract concept in Brehon Consensus documentation should be paired with a concrete example or a visual representation — not as decoration, but as a second encoding channel that doubles retrieval probability.

### 1.4 Concrete-First, Then Abstract: Sequencing Evidence

The optimal instructional sequence — concrete exemplar *before* abstract definition or principle — is supported by research on conceptual learning. Rawson, Thomas & Jacoby (2015) demonstrated experimentally that presenting illustrative concrete examples **before** a declarative concept definition produced significantly better learning and retention than presenting the definition first (or definition alone). They found that examples function as "elaborations" that activate prior knowledge and give the abstract definition something concrete to "attach to" in memory. ([Rawson, Thomas & Jacoby (2015), *Educational Psychology Review* 27: 483–504; DOI 10.1007/s10648-014-9273-3](http://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-014-9273-3))

This aligns with Steven Pinker's account of "classic style" writing in *The Sense of Style* (2014): the writer should act as a guide pointing at concrete things in the world, and lead the reader to a generalisation *through* the particular rather than asserting the generalisation and hoping the reader will fill in the concrete. The classic style "puts the concrete first and the abstract later." ([Harvard Gazette on Pinker, 2012](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/11/exorcising-the-curse-of-knowledge/))

Cognitive load research (Sweller, Paas & van Merriënboer) provides a mechanistic explanation: abstract definitions impose high **intrinsic cognitive load** because they contain many interacting elements with no pre-existing schema. Concrete examples reduce this load by anchoring new relational structures to already-organised long-term memory, allowing the working memory budget to be used for schema construction rather than raw element manipulation. (⚠ Specific load-theory citation for sequencing not independently verified in this search pass; see Sweller et al.'s *Cognitive Load Theory*, 2011, Springer, for the general framework.)

**Practical rules:**
- **Rule of Concrete-First:** Open any explanation of a novel concept with a specific, vivid scenario or example. Define the abstraction only after the audience has a referent.
- **Rule of Dual Coding:** Every abstract term should appear alongside either a visual example, a concrete anecdote, or a comparison to something the audience already knows by touch or sight.
- **Rule of Structural Mapping:** When choosing an analogy, ask: "Does this share the same underlying rules, not just the same surface appearance?" Prefer structurally rich analogies over superficially similar ones.
- **Rule of Explicit Transfer:** Never assume the analogy will do its work silently. Name it. Instruct the audience to use it.

## 2. The Curse of Knowledge

### 2.1 The Core Finding: Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989)

The academic term "curse of knowledge" was introduced in economics by **Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber** in a 1989 paper in the *Journal of Political Economy*. Their central finding — drawn from both individual and market experiments — is that **better-informed agents are systematically unable to ignore their private information, even when it is in their interest to do so.** In their experimental market design, buyers who knew the value of a commodity still failed to adjust their bids as a naïve rational actor would; their known value "bled" into their judgement of others' expected valuations.

Their key conclusion: "more information is not always better" — because having information makes it nearly impossible to simulate the experience of not having it. Market forces, they found, **reduced** the curse by approximately 50 per cent but did **not eliminate it**. Even competitive pricing feedback was insufficient to make experts fully simulate novice perspectives. ([Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989), *Journal of Political Economy* 97(5): 1232–1254; DOI 10.1086/261651](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/261651))

### 2.2 Newton's Tappers and Listeners Study (1990)

The most vividly memorable demonstration of the curse in a communication context is **Elizabeth Newton's 1990 Stanford doctoral dissertation**. Newton divided 240 participants into "tappers" and "listeners." Tappers chose a well-known song (e.g. "Happy Birthday," "The Star-Spangled Banner") from a list of 25 and tapped its rhythm on a table. Listeners had to identify the song from the taps alone. Before the experiment, tappers were asked to predict the proportion of their songs that would be correctly identified.

Results:
- Out of **120 songs tapped**, listeners identified correctly just **3 — a 2.5% success rate**.
- Tappers predicted a **50% success rate** — a 20-fold overestimate.

Why? When tapping, the tappers could *hear* the song in their heads — melody, lyrics, rhythm, all present simultaneously. The listener heard only isolated knocking, "a kind of bizarre Morse code," as Newton described it. The tapper literally could not imagine the listener's impoverished signal, because the full rich song was unavoidably present in working memory throughout the performance. This is the curse of knowledge in pure form: once you know the song, you cannot un-know it, and you radically overestimate how much information your output conveys.

The study is widely cited in popular and scholarly contexts. It appears in Heath & Heath's *Made to Stick* (2007) and is described as "one of the most widely cited demonstrations of the curse of knowledge in communication contexts." ([Newton (1990), unpublished Stanford dissertation — discussed in detail in Heath & Heath; summary at BKW Partners](https://www.bkwpartners.com/tappers-and-listeners-an-excerpt-from-one-of-my-favorite-communications-books-and-a-story-i-tell-clients-often/); [description at TD.org](https://www.td.org/content/td-magazine/break-the-curse-of-knowledge))

### 2.3 Pinker's Cognitive Account in *The Sense of Style* (2014)

Steven Pinker's *The Sense of Style: The Thinking Person's Guide to Writing in the 21st Century* (2014) is the most influential popular-science treatment of the curse of knowledge and its remedies. Pinker explicitly borrows the term from Camerer et al. but extends its scope from economic settings to every communicative act.

His central argument: **"The curse of knowledge is the single best explanation of why good people write bad prose."** It is not, he insists, that experts write obscurely to bamboozle outsiders or signal status — though that exists too. The primary mechanism is more mundane and harder to fix: the expert genuinely does not notice that the intermediate steps, the jargon terms, and the background context that are transparent to them are invisible to the novice. "It simply doesn't occur to the writer that her readers don't know what she knows — that they haven't mastered the missing steps that seem too obvious to mention, have no way to visualise a scene that to her is as clear as day." ([Pinker essay "The Source of Bad Writing", *Wall Street Journal*, September 2014 — reproduced at](https://www.wetenschappelijkschrijven.nl/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Essay-The-Curse-of-Knowledge-by-Steven-Pinker.pdf); [summary at Psychological Science Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/the-curse-of-knowledge-pinker-describes-a-key-cause-of-bad-writing))

Pinker borrows from cognitive psychology to explain the mechanism. Experts encode knowledge in **chunks** — compact, integrated units formed through repeated experience. A novice encountering the same material has no pre-formed chunks; each element must be processed individually, rapidly overloading working memory. The expert who writes "the metacognitive regulatory framework" is deploying a single chunk; the novice must decode it as five or more separate concepts, none of them connected.

**Pinker's evidence-based fixes:**

1. **Classic style over hedged academic style.** The writer should imagine pointing at things in the world the reader can see, rather than building abstraction upon abstraction. "The writer has an obligation to see through the words to see what they represent."

2. **Show drafts to a representative reader.** "You'd often be surprised to find that what's obvious to you is not obvious to anyone else." This is the most reliable cure — external feedback from someone who genuinely lacks your background knowledge.

3. **Revise explicitly for clarity without adding content.** A separate revision pass devoted only to comprehensibility, not to adding new information.

4. **Climb down the "ladder of abstraction."** Replace nominalised, abstract terms ("the implementation of participatory governance mechanisms") with concrete, actor–action–object constructions ("local residents vote on which rules apply in their community").

5. **Eliminate zombie nouns (nominalisations).** "Decide" → "the decision"; "govern" → "governance." Nominalisation hides actors, obscures causation, and is one of the most consistent surface features of the curse.

A related empirical finding from Hinds (1999) strengthens Pinker's account: experts **wildly over-predict how well novices grasp technical content**, and this is not simply a matter of motivation or effort — expertise reorganises the expert's mental landscape so thoroughly that they have "forgotten which stepping-stones they used to climb to their current understanding." ([Discussed in BPS Psychologist review of *Sense of Style*](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/cursed-knowledge))

**Additional evidence:** The Three Mile Island nuclear accident (1979) is cited in *The Sense of Style* as a near-catastrophic consequence of the curse: operators and regulators issued instructions written in language that made perfect sense to nuclear engineers but that were ambiguous or opaque to the operators on the ground who had to act on them under stress.

**Practical remedies summary:**

| Problem caused by curse | Remedy |
|---|---|
| Jargon/unexplained terms | Test on a "cold reader" who lacks your background |
| Skipped logical steps | Write as a chain: A → B → C; show the connective tissue |
| Abstraction without grounding | Pair every abstraction with a concrete example (see Section 1) |
| Nominalised, passive constructions | Rewrite as actor–action–outcome |
| Over-estimating reader knowledge | Assume your reader knows one-third as much as you think |

## 3. Plain Language and Readability Research

### 3.1 The Plain-Language Movement

The plain-language movement has roots in the 1970s US consumer and government reform movements. Key milestones:
- 1972: US President Nixon ordered the *Federal Register* to be written in "layman's terms."
- 1998: US President Clinton's executive memorandum requiring plain language in all government writing.
- 2010: **The Plain Writing Act** made plain language mandatory for all US federal agency communications to the public. ([PlainLanguage.gov guidance, Plain Writing Act of 2010](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/))

In the UK, the **Plain English Campaign** (founded 1979 by Chrissie Maher and Martin Cutts) pioneered plain-language rewriting of government forms and legal documents. The global organisation **Clarity** (founded 1983) focuses specifically on plain legal language.

### 3.2 Kimble's Evidence Compilation: *Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please* (2012)

The most comprehensive single evidence compilation is **Joseph Kimble's** *Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please: The Case for Plain Language in Business, Government, and Law* (Carolina Academic Press, 2012). Kimble, a professor at Thomas M. Cooley Law School and drafting consultant on the US Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and Evidence, summarises **50 empirical studies** showing that plain language:

- Is understood **better and faster** than legalistic or bureaucratic style.
- Results in **fewer errors, fewer callbacks, fewer complaints**.
- Raises **compliance rates** — readers are more likely to act on plain documents.
- **Saves money at scale**: one case study showed that rewriting a Social Security Administration notice in plain language increased the response rate from 43% to **66%**, saving **$4.4 million in 1999 dollars** in staff time.
- In the military context: naval officers who read a business memo written in plain style had **17–23% faster reading times** and significantly higher comprehension than those reading the bureaucratic version. Projected savings for all naval personnel: **$250–$350 million per year**.

A review of Kimble's book describes it as "the one we've been waiting for" and a "game-changer for public communication." ([Kimble (2012), *Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please*, Carolina Academic Press; PDF excerpt via Michigan Bar Journal](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol6_04_Writing_for_Dollars.pdf); [book overview](https://books.google.com/books/about/Writing_for_Dollars_Writing_to_Please.html?id=E-U9LgEACAAJ))

Kimble also debunks ten common myths about plain language — in particular the myth that simplification sacrifices precision. He demonstrates that plain language is typically *more* precise because it requires the writer to resolve ambiguity rather than hide it in vague abstraction.

### 3.3 The Measurable Cost of Jargon: Oppenheimer (2006)

**Daniel M. Oppenheimer** (then Princeton, now Carnegie Mellon) published a landmark five-experiment study in *Applied Cognitive Psychology* with the deliberately ironic title: "Consequences of Erudite Vernacular Utilized Irrespective of Necessity: Problems with Using Long Words Needlessly." The study won an Ig Nobel Prize and is among the most-cited papers in applied psycholinguistics.

**Summary of the five experiments:**

1. **Experiment 1:** Personal statements for graduate admission were rewritten at three levels of vocabulary complexity (using thesaurus substitution). More complex texts led to **lower acceptance rates**, regardless of the underlying quality of the application. Controlling for reading difficulty nearly eliminated the complexity–acceptance relationship, suggesting the mechanism is **processing fluency**.

2. **Experiment 2:** Two English translations of a Descartes paragraph (one simple, one complex) were given to 39 participants, half told the author was Descartes, half told it was anonymous. **Both groups rated the simple-translation author as more intelligent**, regardless of prior expectations.

3. **Experiment 3:** A sociology dissertation abstract was simplified (long words replaced with shorter synonyms). Readers of the simplified version rated the author as **more intelligent** and found it **easier to understand**.

4. **Experiment 4:** Font difficulty (italicised "Juice ITC" vs. Times New Roman) was used to manipulate fluency independently of vocabulary. Hard-to-read font produced **lower intelligence ratings** — establishing that the mechanism is *any* reduction in processing fluency, not vocabulary per se.

5. **Experiment 5:** Obvious, irrelevant causes of disfluency (low-toner printing) caused readers to **discount** their fluency judgements and even over-compensate, rating the low-toner author slightly higher — confirming that fluency is used as a heuristic cue to intelligence, and can be corrected when its source is made salient.

**Overall conclusion (Oppenheimer's words):** "Needless complexity leads to negative evaluations." "Write as simply and plainly as possible and it's more likely you'll be thought of as intelligent." The effect operated across **personal statements, philosophical essays, and sociology abstracts** — three entirely different domains and genres. ([Oppenheimer (2006), *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 20(2): 139–156; DOI 10.1002/acp.1178](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.1178); [PDF via UNM](https://cahill.people.unm.edu/480-21/Oppenheimer-2006-Applied_Cognitive_Psychology.pdf))

This directly contradicts the folk belief — common among civic, legal, and technical writers — that complex vocabulary signals competence or seriousness. The evidence shows the opposite: **simple language signals both intelligence and trustworthiness**, because it is easier to process, and fluency is used as a proxy for credibility.

### 3.4 Shulman et al.: Jargon Reduces Trust, Engagement, and Scientific Interest

**Hilary C. Shulman** (Ohio State University) and colleagues have produced a series of studies establishing that jargon in public communication causes harm well beyond simple comprehension failure. The key 2020 paper in the *Journal of Language and Social Psychology* — co-authored with Graham Dixon, Olivia Bullock, and Daniel Colón Amill — tested N = 650 adults from the general public.

Participants read articles on emerging technologies (self-driving cars, 3D bio-printing, surgical robots) in either jargon-heavy or plain-language versions. The jargon-group not only understood less — they reported:

- **Less interest in science generally** (not just in the specific topic).
- **Lower confidence in their ability to understand science**.
- **Greater "motivated resistance to persuasion"** — i.e. they actively counter-argued against the content they were reading.
- **Increased risk perceptions** and **lower support for technology adoption**.

Crucially: **providing definitions for jargon terms made no difference**. Participants who read jargon with glossary definitions were just as disengaged as those who read unexplained jargon. The authors interpret this as evidence that jargon functions as a **social identity signal**: the mere presence of technical language tells the reader "this is not for you" and triggers social exclusion before any comprehension processing occurs.

Shulman et al.'s interpretation: "Jargon can serve as exclusionary language that disengages meaningful relationships between public and expert communities from forming." ([Shulman et al. (2020), *Journal of Language and Social Psychology*; DOI 10.1177/0261927x20902177](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0261927x20902177); [summary at Advanced Science News](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/jargon-kills-peoples-interest-in-science/); [open-access companion paper "Jargon as a barrier to effective science communication," Bullock et al., via OSU](https://comm.osu.edu/sites/comm.osu.edu/files/PUS%202019-%20Bullock%20et%20al..pdf))

A companion study (Shulman et al., 2021, as cited in subsequent literature) replicated these findings across COVID-19 communication and emergency policy, finding that jargon impairs persuasion in **non-crisis** contexts (when motivation to process is low) but that high situational urgency can partially override this effect. The practical implication for governance communications: since grassroots civic contexts are generally *low-urgency* for most participants, **jargon is almost always counter-productive**. ([PMC open-access version of Shulman et al. COVID study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540871/))

The *Nature* editorial (2020) draws on this research to state plainly that "words matter: jargon alienates readers" and calls on researchers and institutions to audit their public-facing language. ([Nature, Feb 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-020-00580-w))

### 3.5 Morkes & Nielsen (1997): Web Readability Study

**John Morkes and Jakob Nielsen** conducted one of the most-cited empirical studies of writing style and usability. Testing five different writing styles for the same web content, they measured usability scores across time-on-task, errors, memory, and site navigation. Relative to the control (promotional style):

- **Concise writing** (about half the word count): **+58% usability**
- **Scannable layout** (headings, bullets, bold keywords): **+47% usability**
- **Objective language** (no marketing puffery): **+27% usability**
- **All three combined**: **+124% usability**

This study is foundational in web content strategy and remains highly influential in civic and government digital communications. ([Morkes & Nielsen (1997), "Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web"; archived at NN/G](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/); [NN/G "How Users Read on the Web"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/))

**Practical rules for Brehon Consensus:**
1. **Use plain vocabulary**: prefer everyday words; test every technical term with a cold reader.
2. **Provide context before jargon, not just definitions**: the issue is social signalling, not vocabulary.
3. **Eliminate nominalisation and passive constructions**: "You vote on rules" not "The participatory governance mechanism facilitates rule-setting."
4. **Use concrete examples to anchor every abstract concept** (dual coding principle).
5. **Never rely on a glossary as the fix for jargon**: Shulman's data show it changes nothing for engagement.
6. **Halve your word count**: Morkes & Nielsen's conciseness finding suggests the average communicator over-writes by roughly 100%.

## 4. Information Chunking and Progressive Disclosure

### 4.1 Miller's 7±2 (1956) — and Why It Has Been Revised

**George A. Miller's** 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two: Some Limits on Our Capacity for Processing Information" in *Psychological Review* is one of the most cited papers in psychology. Miller noticed a convergence across diverse tasks (digit span, tonal discrimination, colour discrimination) around a capacity of approximately 7 ± 2 "chunks." ([Miller (1956), *Psychological Review* 63(2): 81–97; DOI 10.1037/h0043158](https://doi.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0043158))

However, **Miller himself** was cautious: "That number was meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit." The famous figure has been widely misapplied in design and communications guidance. As Nelson Cowan noted in a retrospective, Miller's experiments conflated the effects of rehearsal, chunking, and long-term memory access with pure short-term capacity. ([Cowan (2015), *Psychological Review* 122(3): 536–543; DOI 10.1037/a0039035](https://doi.apa.org/doi/10.1037/a0039035))

### 4.2 Cowan's 4±1: The Updated Limit

The current scientific consensus, established by **Nelson Cowan** (University of Missouri), is that the genuine capacity limit of the **focus of attention** — i.e. the number of chunks that can be held simultaneously in an active, accessible state without rehearsal — is approximately **3 to 5 chunks, with a central estimate of about 4**. Cowan's 2001 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* target article brought together dozens of converging data sources: visual arrays, prose recall, working-memory discontinuities, and indirect effects of the limit.

The abstract: "A single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks is implicated along with other, noncapacity-limited sources." The paper has been cited over 4,300 times. ([Cowan (2001), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24(1): 87–114; DOI 10.1017/S0140525X01003922](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0140525X01003922/type/journal_article))

Cowan's subsequent review in *Current Directions in Psychological Science* (2010) clarified the conditions under which this 3–5 chunk limit is observable and why it matters for information processing. ([Cowan (2010), *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 19(1): 51–57; DOI 10.1177/0963721409359277](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721409359277))

**Key implication:** The difference between 7 and 4 is practically significant for document and interface design. If a reader can only hold about **4 chunks** in active attention at once, a navigation menu, a list of principles, or a set of steps that runs to seven or more items will exceed capacity and force a trade-off: some items will be dropped from active processing, or the entire set will be processed more shallowly. **Lists of three to five items are not just aesthetic convention — they are cognitively grounded.**

### 4.3 Chunking in Practice: Design Principles

Miller's more lasting contribution is not the specific number but the concept of **chunking itself**: the process by which familiar sequences or relationships are bundled into single, retrievable units. Experts handle complexity primarily by having *larger and more numerous chunks* — not larger working-memory slots. This means that **presenting information in pre-chunked form** (grouped, labelled, and logically bounded) dramatically reduces the processing load on novice audiences who lack the expert's pre-built chunks.

Pinker's discussion in *The Sense of Style* (Ch. 3) ties this directly to the curse of knowledge: experts communicate in chunks that only other experts can decode; the curse is invisible because the expert cannot experience what it is like to lack those chunks. ([BPS Psychologist review of Pinker](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/cursed-knowledge))

**Practical chunking rules:**
- **Limit any single list, set of steps, or menu to 4–5 items**. If more are needed, group them into super-chunks with their own labels.
- **Name each chunk clearly** before enumerating its contents.
- **Lead with the chunk label**, not its members: "There are three ways to participate: [A], [B], [C]" — not "[A], [B], [C] are the three ways to participate."
- **Group related items**. Random or unrelated lists are the hardest to chunk; logical grouping turns a list of 9 into three groups of 3.

### 4.4 Progressive Disclosure

**Progressive disclosure** is a design principle formalised by **Jakob Nielsen** (Nielsen Norman Group, 1995–2006) to manage the tension between two competing user needs: power/features vs. simplicity. The resolution: **show users only the most important information or options initially; offer additional detail only on explicit request.**

From Nielsen's canonical NN/G article: "Initially, show users only a few of the most important options. Offer a larger set of specialised options upon request. Disclose these secondary features only if a user asks for them, meaning that most users can proceed with their tasks without worrying about this added complexity." Progressive disclosure improves **three of usability's five components: learnability, efficiency of use, and error rate**. ([Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure," NN/G (originally 1995, revised 2022)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/))

A key counter-intuitive finding from Nielsen's research: "People understand a system *better* when you help them prioritise features and spend more time on the most important ones" — even though one might assume that seeing fewer features would give them a more limited mental model. The opposite is true: cognitive overload from full feature exposure at once actually impedes model formation.

**Critical design constraint:** "In practice, designs that go beyond **2 disclosure levels** typically have low usability because users often get lost when moving between the levels." For documents, landing pages, or leaflets, this translates to: a **summary layer** (what this is and why it matters) and a **detail layer** (how it works and what to do) — and nothing more complex than that for non-technical audiences.

The underlying principle — articulated by Ben Shneiderman (1996) as the **"Visual Information-Seeking Mantra"**: *"Overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand"* — applies equally to information visualisation, document architecture, and civic communication. Start with the shape; let the reader choose their depth.

### 4.5 Message Ladders and Layered Messaging

The communications strategy concept of **"message ladders"** or **layered messaging** operationalises progressive disclosure for advocacy and civic contexts. A message ladder has multiple rungs, each a deeper version of the same core claim, calibrated to different audience types:

- **Top rung (3–5 words):** The slogan or headline — so short a child can remember it.
- **Middle rung (1–2 sentences):** The elevator pitch — for a passer-by or a social media caption.
- **Lower rung (1 paragraph):** The substantive explanation — for someone who asks "tell me more."
- **Bottom rung (full document):** The detailed case — for the person who wants evidence and specifics.

This structure ensures that **everyone gets the core message regardless of how much they read**, and that more-engaged readers can access detail without the overview being cluttered with it.

The message ladder is widely used in non-profit and government communications. As Prosper Strategies' guidance notes, "master key messages" should be usable across all audiences, with audience-specific supporting messages layered below. ([Prosper Strategies, "Writing Effective Key Messages"](https://prosper-strategies.com/nonprofit-messaging-key-messages/))

### 4.6 The Inverted Pyramid

The **inverted pyramid** is the canonical journalistic writing structure, dating to AP wire services in the mid-19th century. The metaphor: a wide triangle balanced on its point, with the broadest and most essential information at the top, narrowing to the least essential at the bottom.

- **Lead paragraph:** The core message, answering Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How (the "Five Ws + H") in one to three sentences — the single most important thing, stated immediately.
- **Body:** Supporting evidence, context, quotations, ranked by likely reader interest, in descending order.
- **Tail:** Background, caveats, additional resources — material that can be cut without losing the story.

The structure serves two audiences simultaneously: the reader who stops after one paragraph has the essential information; the reader who reads to the end has all the detail. **It can be cut at any point** without losing coherence.

Nielsen's NN/G research confirms the inverted pyramid is "perfectly suited for the web," because users read in **F-shaped patterns** — heavy attention to the first line, lighter attention to subsequent lines, with a final vertical scan down the left margin. Front-loaded content reaches both the skimmer and the reader. ([NN/G, "Inverted Pyramid: Writing for Comprehension" (2018)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/))

Morkes & Nielsen's 1997 study confirms this empirically: qualitative participants said things like "I was able to find the main point quickly, from the first line. I like that." The inverted pyramid is not just a professional convention; it is a cognitive match to how people actually process information under real-world conditions of partial attention.

**Integrated practical framework for Brehon Consensus documentation:**

| Layer | Format | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Layer 0 (headline)** | 3–5 words | What this does for you |
| **Layer 1 (lead)** | 1–3 sentences | Who, what, why — the essential claim |
| **Layer 2 (summary)** | 3–5 bullet points | The key principles or steps, each ≤ one line |
| **Layer 3 (explanation)** | Short paragraphs, ≤4 points per section | Evidence, examples, context — for engaged readers |
| **Layer 4 (full detail)** | Linked document | Technical specification, edge cases, governance rules |

Design rules:
1. **Every page or section must stand alone**: anyone who reads only Layer 0–1 must understand the core message.
2. **Maximum 4–5 items per list or section** (Cowan's limit).
3. **Maximum 2 progressive-disclosure levels** in any single interaction or document (Nielsen's constraint).
4. **Concrete example before abstract principle** in every Layer 3 explanation (concrete-first rule).
5. **No unexplained jargon at Layers 0–2** (Shulman's exclusion-effect finding).

## B1 — Framing Theory for Social and Political Communication

### B1.1 — The Core Claim: Lead with Values, Not Mechanics

The foundational insight of the framing tradition is that audiences do not evaluate ideas as neutral propositions; they filter incoming information through pre-existing mental frameworks — cognitive structures acquired through culture, experience, and repeated exposure — that determine what "makes sense" and what does not. For civic technology aimed at grassroots organisers, this has a direct practical implication: leading with the mechanics of how a governance tool works ("it uses a ranked-choice vote with a quorum rule") will be far less effective than leading with the values the tool enacts ("it makes sure every voice in your group counts equally").

---

### B1.2 — George Lakoff: Cognitive Frames and the Limits of Factual Persuasion

**Core argument.** George Lakoff, cognitive linguist at the University of California Berkeley, argues in *Don't Think of an Elephant!* (Chelsea Green, 2004; expanded 2014) and *Moral Politics* (University of Chicago Press, 1996/2002) that "frames are mental structures that shape the way we see the world." Every word activates a frame — a network of associated ideas, values, and expectations physically encoded in neural circuitry. Crucially, when you negate a frame, you reinforce it: telling voters "this is not a tax rise" activates the frame of a tax rise. The strategic implication is stark: **never use the opponent's language; never argue on their terms**. Progressive campaigns that begin by reciting conservative terminology (e.g. "the death tax is not really a death tax") have already conceded the conceptual terrain.

Lakoff's deeper argument, set out most fully in *Moral Politics*, is that American political division maps onto two idealised family models. Conservative positions cohere around a "strict father" morality — authority, discipline, self-reliance, punishment for transgression. Progressive positions cohere around a "nurturant parent" morality — empathy, shared responsibility, cooperation, and care. He identifies what he calls "biconceptuals" — people who hold both moral systems, applying each to different domains — as the key persuadable constituency. The strategic goal is not to change minds but to activate the nurturant frame in biconceptual voters through consistent progressive language, rather than moving towards the other side, which only strengthens its neural circuitry. He distils practical guidance into four imperatives: show respect; respond by reframing; think and talk at the level of values; say what you believe rather than listing policies ([George Lakoff, "In Politics, Progressives Need to Frame Their Values," Truthout interview, 2014](https://george-lakoff.com/2014/11/29/george-lakoff-in-politics-progressives-need-to-frame-their-values/)).

The famous classroom exercise from which the book takes its title — "Don't think of an elephant!" — illustrates the basic mechanism: every word evokes its associated frame, even in negation. ["Framing 101: How to Take Back Public Discourse," Lakoff lecture notes, andrelevy.net](https://andrelevy.net/lakoff_framing101.pdf).

A summary of the key principles is available in [*Frame the Debate: Insights from Don't Think of an Elephant!*](https://commonslibrary.org/frame-the-debate-insights-from-dont-think-of-an-elephant/), The Commons Social Change Library (2020).

**Application to Brehon Consensus.** A governance tool described in terms of "modules," "deployment," or "fork this repository" will be immediately filtered through a "technical complexity" or "this isn't for me" frame. Framing the same tool around values — fairness, accountability, the right of every member to have a say — activates cognitive structures that non-technical organisers already hold positively.

---

**Scholarly critiques of Lakoff's empirical basis.** Lakoff's framework has attracted significant academic criticism on several grounds:

1. **Weak empirical grounding.** By Lakoff's own admission, the Strict Father / Nurturant Parent hypothesis in *Moral Politics* was derived from observation and theoretical reasoning rather than systematic empirical testing. A DePaul University thesis (Myers, 2016) designed a computational algorithm to test Lakoff's metaphor predictions against 2016 presidential candidate speeches and found that "Republicans used Strict Father metaphors more than Democrats as expected, but the difference was slight" — and that "the empirical test of Lakoff's model in *Moral Politics* was not supported by this study" — though the study acknowledged limitations in metaphor identification methodology ([Myers, "Speaking Presidentially," DePaul University Academics, 2016](https://academics.depaul.edu/honors/curriculum/Documents/2016%20Senior%20Theses/Myers,%20Vladimir%20Senior%20Thesis%20SQ15-16.pdf)).

2. **Reductionist neural metaphor.** A 2022 paper by Diodato (Sapienza University) argues that Lakoff's claim that ideological frames are "physically in our brains" amounts to "an outdated, reductionist communication model" rooted in first- and second-generation cognitive science, critiquing it as essentially a neural translation of classical propaganda theory that fails to account for the complexity of political communication ([Diodato, "Chomsky-Lakoff," Iris Uniroma, 2022](https://iris.uniroma1.it/retrieve/457fa233-c3ef-42d4-9e27-a56265cb35eb/Diodato_Chomsky-Lakoff_2022.pdf)).

3. **Methodological rigour.** An empirical study by Jaspaert et al. (2011) in *Cognitive Linguistics* specifically tested whether Lakoff-derived "Simplifying Models" shifted cognitive construal in a controlled experiment on sustainable food production. They found framing did produce measurable effects, but cautioned that "existing studies on framing in linguistics have either been theoretical or descriptive" and that more rigorous test designs often yield "a more reliable image of the effect" than Lakoff's popularised accounts suggest ([Jaspaert et al., *Cognitive Linguistics* 22(3), 2011](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/bitstream/handle/123456789/306963/JaspaertEtAl_2011_CL.pdf)).

4. **Political efficacy questioned.** Critics note that despite the widespread adoption of Lakoff's advice by progressive organisations throughout the 2000s and 2010s, the framing gap has not obviously closed. The strategic value of "repeating your own frame" is hard to distinguish empirically from simple repetition effects.

**Bottom line.** Lakoff's framework offers useful heuristics — especially the injunction to lead with values and never reinforce an opponent's frame through negation — but practitioners should treat his neural and psychological claims with caution. The prescriptions are more robustly supported by the wider framing and social cognition literature than by the specific empirical evidence Lakoff himself adduces.

### B1.3 — The FrameWorks Institute: Strategic Frame Analysis™

The FrameWorks Institute (Washington DC, founded 1999) has developed what is arguably the most rigorous empirical programme for testing framing in public communication. Their trademarked "Strategic Frame Analysis™" (SFA) is a multi-method iterative process — combining survey research, semi-structured interviews, focus groups, media content analysis, and experimental message effects tests — that moves from descriptive research (what frames currently govern public thinking on an issue?) to prescriptive research (which alternative frames measurably shift public reasoning towards expert-aligned understanding and policy support?).

**Strategic Frame Analysis described.** FrameWorks defines a frame as "a bundle of different associations activated in the brain by a cue" that allows an audience to interpret a message by evoking particular cognitive models. The analytic goal is to identify "dominant frames" — those that shape media and public discourse — and to develop and experimentally test "reframes" that shift interpretation ([FrameWorks Institute, "Framing Public Issues" toolkit, 2002, PDF](https://www.frameworksinstitute.org/app/uploads/2020/07/FramingPublicIssuesfinal.pdf)):

> "When communication is adequate, people can see an issue from a different perspective. In this way, the challenge of communications becomes reframing — providing a different lens for the processing of new information."

**Redirecting the gaze.** A key concept in FrameWorks practice is what they call "redirecting the gaze" — strategically moving public attention away from unproductive default thinking patterns (individualism, fatalism, "separate fates") towards more systemic or collective understandings. For example, their research on child mental health found that the dominant frame was hyper-individualistic (the child's personal problem, the parent's failure); the reframe they developed used the value of "Human Potential" paired with a metaphor of "Levelness" — *just as the levelness of a table makes it functional, the mental health of children is what enables them to function well in all areas of life* — to redirect towards systemic explanations and policy solutions ([FrameWorks Institute, "Using Strategic Frame Analysis to Talk," webinar PDF, 2020](https://www.frameworksinstitute.org/app/uploads/2020/06/evergreenwebinar.pdf)).

**Explanatory Metaphors.** FrameWorks defines Explanatory Metaphors as "research-driven, empirically tested analogies that capture and distil a concept through reference to existing patterns of assumption and understanding." They serve four functions: *clarifying* (helping people understand a process), *channelling* (nudging thinking in directions that allow new information to be incorporated), *inoculating* (preventing unproductive frames from dominating), and *stickiness* (being memorable and communicable). Examples include "Justice Maze" and "Justice Gears" for the criminal justice system, tested with approximately 1,300 members of the general public ([FrameWorks Institute, "Maze and Gears," 2024](https://www.frameworksinstitute.org/resources/maze-and-gears-using-explanatory-metaphors-to-increase-public-understanding-of-the-criminal-justice-system-and-its-reform/)).

Their metaphor research PDF states: "Researchers were greatly impressed by the power of these cognitive tools to quickly redirect conversations about the criminal justice system toward the kinds of policies and programs that experts identified" ([FrameWorks Institute, Maze and Gears PDF, 2020](https://www.frameworksinstitute.org/app/uploads/2020/03/pscj_metaphors.pdf)).

**Basic message template.** FrameWorks recommends a three-level message structure:
1. Start with a **value** (Level 1) — e.g. "Community: we are stronger when we make decisions together."
2. **Explain the issue** using an explanatory metaphor (Level 2).
3. **Introduce the solution** (Level 3) — the tool, policy, or behaviour change.

This structure maps well onto Brehon Consensus: the value (democratic self-governance), the metaphor (perhaps a "shared rule-book" or "community constitution"), and the solution (the open-source governance layer).

### B1.4 — The Common Cause Foundation: Schwartz's Values Circumplex and Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Values

**The Schwartz model.** Social psychologist Shalom Schwartz (Hebrew University of Jerusalem) established through cross-cultural research in more than 60 countries that human values cluster into ten motivational groups arranged on a circumplex (circular) model, organised along two major axes: *Self-Enhancement* (power, achievement) versus *Self-Transcendence* (universalism, benevolence); and *Openness to Change* (self-direction, stimulation) versus *Conservation* (tradition, conformity, security). Critically, values at adjacent positions on the circumplex are compatible and mutually reinforcing; values at opposing positions are in tension and suppress one another ([Common Cause Handbook, Public Interest Research Centre, 2012, commoncausefoundation.org](https://commoncausefoundation.org/_resources/the-common-cause-handbook/), [PDF at publicinterest.org.uk](https://publicinterest.org.uk/download/values/Common%20Cause%20Handbook.pdf)).

**Intrinsic versus extrinsic values.** The Common Cause framework — developed by Tom Crompton at WWF-UK, drawing on Schwartz and on the psychologists Richard Ryan and Tim Kasser — maps the circumplex onto a further distinction between *intrinsic values* (inherently rewarding to pursue: community feeling, self-acceptance, equality, creativity, connection with nature) and *extrinsic values* (contingent on external validation: wealth, material success, image, social status, power). The *Common Cause Handbook* (PIRC, 2012), commissioned by WWF-UK, Oxfam, and Action for Children, synthesises extensive social-psychological evidence:

- Prioritising intrinsic values is "closely related to political engagement, concern about social justice, environmentally-friendly behaviours, and lower levels of prejudice."
- Prioritising extrinsic values is "generally associated with higher levels of prejudice; less concern about the environment and corresponding behaviours; weak (or absent) concern about human rights; more manipulative behaviour and less helpfulness."
- Extrinsic values also "tend to undermine our levels of personal wellbeing."

The *No Cause is an Island* report (Common Cause Foundation, 2021) reports experimental evidence that messages invoking intrinsic, self-transcendent values produce greater expressed intention to take action on social or environmental causes **regardless of whether the respondent already holds those values** — even participants "relatively more disposed toward self-enhancement values were more likely to report an intention to take action" when presented with an intrinsic message ([Common Cause Foundation, "No Cause is an Island," 2021, PDF](https://commoncausefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/CCF_report_no_cause_is_an_island.pdf)). The effect size for the comparison between intrinsic and mixed (intrinsic + extrinsic) primes was β = 0.21, though non-significant in that study, suggesting more research is needed on mixed priming.

The *Common Cause Handbook* also notes a "contamination" risk: mixing intrinsic and extrinsic appeals in the same message appears to be no more effective than an extrinsic message alone, because extrinsic primes suppress intrinsic motivation. This has direct implications for Brehon Consensus messaging: appeals to efficiency savings or competitive advantage (extrinsic) should not be combined in the same message with appeals to fairness and community (intrinsic).

**The original report.** Tom Crompton's original "Common Cause" report for WWF-UK (2010) argued that civil society campaigns were making a systematic strategic error by appealing to self-interest and extrinsic motivations in order to "meet people where they are," and that this approach was inadvertently reinforcing the very values that undermine collective action ([PIRC/WWF "Think of me as Evil?" PDF, assets.wwf.org.uk](http://assets.wwf.org.uk/downloads/think_of_me_as_evil.pdf)).

**Critique of Common Cause.** The Green House Think Tank published a detailed critique (2016) arguing that the Common Cause conflation of Schwartz's values circumplex (which is a theory of values as motivational goals) with Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory (SDT) intrinsic/extrinsic motivation continuum is conceptually untidy: "The concept of intrinsic and extrinsic values/motivations belongs to SDT, which posits that intrinsic motivations are inherently rewarding... SDT's values work along a *continuum*, from extrinsic to intrinsic, in which the two extremes are characterised by a lack of agency and full agency respectively" — rather than operating around a circumplex as Schwartz's values do. The critique also challenges whether the value-change case is empirically demonstrated versus correlation ([Green House Think Tank, "Critiquing Common Cause," 2016](https://www.greenhousethinktank.org/static/2016/critiquing_common_cause_v1.pdf)).

**Implications for Brehon Consensus.** Frame the product around intrinsic values: democratic participation, equal voice, community self-governance, solidarity, fairness. Avoid framing it around productivity, efficiency, growth, competitive advantage, or scale — even if true, these activate extrinsic value frames that suppress engagement with the deeper civic purpose.

## B2 — Narrative and Story vs Bullet-Point Facts for Persuasion and Retention

### B2.1 — Why Stories Beat Facts: The Cognitive Architecture

The preference for narrative is not a cultural prejudice; it reflects deep features of human cognition. Neuroscientist Uri Hasson's work at Princeton has shown that when a speaker tells a story, the listener's brain activity synchronises with the speaker's (neural coupling); information delivered as bullet-point lists does not produce the same synchrony. However, the most directly applicable body of research for communication practice is the narrative transportation literature.

---

### B2.2 — Narrative Transportation Theory (Green & Brock, 2000)

Melanie Green and Timothy Brock introduced the concept of "narrative transportation" in a landmark 2000 paper in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* — "The Role of Transportation in the Persuasiveness of Public Narratives" (*J. Pers. Soc. Psychol.* 79, 701–721, 2000). The original citation is confirmed in multiple subsequent studies ([confirmed in Kawabata et al., 2015, citing "Green, M. C., & Brock, T. C. (2000). The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 79, 701–721"](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/pacjpa/79/0/79_3PM-026/_article/-char/ja/)).

Transportation is defined as "a combination of attention, imagery, and feelings, in which an individual becomes immersed in a narrative world" ([Green, "Transportation into Narrative Worlds," chapter in *Entertainment-Education Behind the Scenes*, Springer, 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-030-63614-2_6)). The mechanism of persuasion is distinct from standard dual-process models (Elaboration Likelihood Model, Heuristic-Systematic Model): transported readers are *less* likely to engage in counter-arguing, not because they are not processing the message, but because they are immersed in the world of the story and temporarily suspend critical evaluation. Green notes: "transportation increases the susceptibility of the recipients' beliefs to the information presented in the narrative along with potentially diminishing counter-arguments and critical thinking" ([cited in Naseer et al., 2025, *SSHRR*](https://sshrr.online/index.php/4/article/view/330)).

A subsequent meta-analysis by Van Laer et al. confirmed the effect across health, consumer, and social issue contexts. A 2021 replication study found that positive affect mediates narrative transportation — it is the emotional tone of immersion, not genre per se, that drives the effect: "positive affect, but not negative affect, mediated the effect of challenging versus easy text conditions on narrative transportation" ([Isakoglou et al., *Psi Chi Journal*, 2021](https://www.psichi.org/page/264JNWinter2021)).

**Practical upshot.** Even a short, vivid account of a specific mutual aid group that used Brehon Consensus to resolve a dispute fairly will be more persuasive, and better retained, than a feature list or an abstract explanation of the governance model.

---

### B2.3 — Kurt Vonnegut's Shapes of Stories

Novelist and essayist Kurt Vonnegut proposed in his master's thesis (rejected by the University of Chicago) and in later public lectures and in *A Man Without a Country* (2005) that all stories can be represented as emotional trajectories plotted on two axes: *time* (horizontal: beginning to end) and *fortune* (vertical: ill fortune to good fortune). He identified a small set of recurrent shapes:

- **Man in a Hole**: character begins well, encounters serious trouble, recovers. "A story we can't get enough of" — Vonnegut. Cornell University analysis of 6,147 film scripts found this arc earned the highest box office on average of all narrative structures.
- **Boy Meets Girl**: character gains something wonderful, loses it, wins it back.
- **Cinderella**: slow rise, catastrophic fall at midnight, off-the-scale happiness thereafter — "every time someone tells it, they make a million dollars," according to Vonnegut.
- **From Bad to Worse**: continuous descent, as in Kafka.
- **Good News or Bad News** (Hamlet): no clear valence arc; the truest to life but hardest to execute.

These have since been empirically confirmed. Andrew Reagan et al. (2016) analysed 1,737 fiction texts from Project Gutenberg using sentiment analysis and identified six dominant emotional arc shapes — closely matching Vonnegut's taxonomy — through singular value decomposition and hierarchical clustering, finding that stories with more complex arcs ("Man in a Hole," "Icarus," "Cinderella") circulate more intensively on social media than simple monotonic arcs. The paper concluded: "The emotional arcs of a large body of stories reveal a set of basic forms common across cultures and eras." ([Analysed in Ayo, "Vonnegut's Shape of Stories: Why Even Computers Understand Them," *Philarchive*, 2025](https://philarchive.org/archive/AYOVSO))

**Story Spine.** The "Story Spine" (a narrative brainstorming tool developed by improv practitioner Kenn Adams and popularised by Pixar screenwriters) provides a practical scaffold:
> "Once upon a time… Every day… Until one day… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then…"

This structure maps directly onto the "Man in a Hole" or "Challenge Plot" arcs and is useful for training organisers to tell their community's story in a compelling way. The spine forces identification of a *status quo*, a *disruption*, a *consequence chain*, and a *resolution* — all four of which need to be present for a story to function persuasively.

---

### B2.4 — Heath Brothers: *Made to Stick* (2007)

Chip Heath (Stanford Graduate School of Business) and Dan Heath synthesised memory and communication research into a framework for "sticky" ideas in *Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die* (Random House, 2007) ([Stanford GSB page](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/books/made-stick-why-some-ideas-survive-others-die)). Their **SUCCES\*** framework (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credentialed, Emotional, Story) identifies the features that make ideas remembered and acted upon. Their evidence base includes:

**The statistics vs stories experiment.** Chip Heath ran a classroom exercise at Stanford in which students gave one-minute persuasive speeches. "In an average one-minute speech, the typical student uses 2.5 statistics. Only one student in ten tells a story. When students are asked to recall the speeches, **63% remember the stories. Only 5% remember any individual statistic**." ([Made to Stick distillation, Squarespace PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e98f5e53734495f407ec606/t/5ffcb226f84da9486d0fa725/1610396199432/SHOS.Made+to+Stick.Full+Distillation+-+Google+Docs.pdf))

Their key claim on statistics: "Statistics are rarely meaningful in and of themselves. Statistics should be used to illustrate a relationship. It's more important for people to remember the relationship than the number." And on stories: "Stories illustrate causal relationships that people hadn't recognised before and highlight unexpected, resourceful ways in which people have solved problems." The Heath brothers identify three productive story types: **Challenge plots** (overcoming daunting obstacles — most useful for activation), **Connection plots** (bridging social divides — useful for inclusion messaging), and **Creativity plots** (a mental breakthrough — useful for "new possibility" framing).

The Heaths are practitioners and synthesisers rather than primary researchers; their "Made to Stick" framework is not peer-reviewed research. However, the 63%/5% statistic draws on research by Chip Heath in controlled classroom conditions. The broader claim that stories outperform statistics for retention is well-supported in the academic literature on narrative transportation and memory ([Heath Brothers, "Made to Stick: Introduction," heathbrothers.com](https://heathbrothers.com/made-to-stick-introduction/)).

### B2.5 — The Identifiable Victim Effect (Small, Loewenstein, and Slovic, 2007)

Deborah Small, George Loewenstein, and Paul Slovic published a landmark study in the *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty* (2007) demonstrating that people donate more money to help a single identified victim — a child with a name and photograph — than to statistical descriptions of millions in need. In one key experiment, providing statistical information *alongside* an identified victim actually reduced charitable giving compared to the identified victim alone, suggesting that activating deliberative thinking about aggregate statistics suppresses the emotional response generated by a specific individual. This "compassion fade" with increasing numbers of victims is related to what Slovic has elsewhere called "psychic numbing."

The effect has been extensively cited in communication and fundraising practice. However, recent replication attempts have cast doubt on its robustness:

- A 2023 pre-registered replication and extension of Studies 1 and 3 in Small et al. (2007) by Maier, Wong, and Feldman (*Collabra: Psychology*, 2023) found "no empirical support for the identifiable victim effect (ηp² = .000, 95% CI [.000, .003]) and subsequently no support for debiasing such a phenomenon (ηp² = .001, 95% CI [.000, .012])." They noted that their study used hypothetical rather than real donations and called for high-powered replications. They suggest the phenomenon "may be better framed in terms of 'scope-insensitivity' — rather than providing more to a single identified victim, participants seem to be insensitive to the number of victims affected." ([Maier, Wong & Feldman, *Collabra: Psychology*, 2023, DOI: 10.1525/collabra.90203](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/9/1/90203/199223/Revisiting-and-Rethinking-the-Identifiable-Victim))
- A 2018 study by Lane, Hart, and Chinn (*PLOS ONE*, DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0199535) with N = 924 found no significant difference between identified individual, identified group, statistics, and statistics-plus-individual conditions ([Lane et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6051573/)).
- A 2024 study by Västfjäll, Moche, and Karlsson (*PLOS ONE*, DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0300863) found that "the level of identifiability influences affective reactions, this effect does not extend to donations" ([Västfjäll et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10977801/)).

**⚠ Assessment.** The identifiable victim effect in its original strong form — that identified individuals reliably out-fundraise statistical accounts — is contested and has failed independent pre-registered replication. The core insight that *specific stories engage emotion better than statistics* remains broadly supported by the narrative transportation literature, but practitioners should not rely on the "one vs many" mechanism as a dependable effect. Scope insensitivity (people give the same to 1 or 1,000 victims) is probably more robust than the specific directionality of the original effect.

**Practical guidance.** For Brehon Consensus, tell the story of a specific group — a particular workers' co-op, a named mutual aid network — that benefited concretely from better collective decision-making. Describe the specific moment of decision, the specific conflict resolved, the specific outcome. This engages narrative transportation and emotional connection regardless of whether the strict identifiable victim mechanism holds.

## B3 — Messaging an Alternative to a Dominant Status Quo Without Triggering Defensiveness

### B3.1 — The Problem: TINA and the Politics of "No Alternative"

"There Is No Alternative" (TINA) is a political logic most closely associated with Margaret Thatcher's neoliberal programme of the 1980s, though its lineage traces back to Herbert Spencer in the nineteenth century. The phrase and the logic argue that existing social and economic arrangements are not merely the prevailing system but the *only viable* one — that alternatives would lead to catastrophe. TINA functions less as an argument than as a frame: it pre-emptively forecloses the imaginative space in which alternatives might be conceived ([Wikipedia, "There is No Alternative"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_is_no_alternative)).

The rhetorical power of TINA lies in its fusion of descriptive and normative claims: it presents a political choice as a factual necessity, a social construction as a natural law. Research on populism generally agrees that the "disappearance of political discord" associated with TINA's dominance — the acceptance by mainstream parties of certain concepts as "unalterable truths" — contributed significantly to the rise of authoritarian populist alternatives in the 1990s–2020s. TINA allows decisions to appear "not as a political choice, but as a matter of adherence to universal truth and common sense," making the policy "politically paternalistic" ([Wikipedia, ibid.]).

---

### B3.2 — Counter-Narratives and the TALA/TAPAS Frame

**TALA.** "There Are Lots of Alternatives" (TALA) is used by some critical policy scholars as the direct counter to TINA.

**TAPAS.** David Bollier, commons scholar, coined "There Are Plenty of Alternatives" (TAPAS) at a 2014 conclave of internationally-minded activists exploring the commons as an alternative paradigm. The [*Shareable* account of that conclave](https://www.shareable.net/new-report-explores-the-commons-as-a-paradigm-to-align-movements/) notes: "While Margaret Thatcher had once insisted 'There Is No Alternative' to her neoliberal agenda — inspiring the acronym TINA — the takeaway from this conclave of activists was quite the opposite: 'There Are Plenty of Alternatives,' or TAPAS." The conclave identified co-operatives, community finance, commons-based models (community land trusts, participatory budgeting), and open-design communities as instantiations of the TAPAS frame.

TAPAS has subsequently been taken up in education alternatives discourse; a 2025 conference presentation at the Comparative and International Education Society explored "Not TINA but TAPAS!" as a framing device for non-reformist educational alternatives ([convention2.allacademic.com, CIES 2025](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/cies/cies25/index.php?program_focus=view_paper&selected_paper_id=2206847&cmd=online_program_direct_link&sub_action=online_program)).

The Narrative Initiative's 2019 report ["Neoliberalism is Dying: What Narratives Can Replace It?"](https://narrativeinitiative.org/blog/neoliberalism-is-dying-what-narratives-can-replace-it/) identifies the communication challenge: no progressive alternative has yet achieved the "narrative cogency and power" of neoliberalism. Key prescriptions include: *denaturalising* the market (emphasising that markets are structured by intentional policy decisions, not laws of nature); *elevating positive normative visions* (human flourishing, community, dignity) before introducing mechanism; and building narrative coalitions across movements.

**Application to Brehon Consensus.** The project is, in a meaningful sense, a TAPAS intervention: it makes it *visible and tangible* that communities can self-govern without either corporate platforms or state bureaucracy. The messaging should make this concrete: "Here is a group of people who did it. Here is how. Here is the tool they used." This directly counters the TINA logic ("you need big tech, or you need nothing") through existence proofs rather than abstract counter-argument.

### B3.3 — System Justification Theory (Jost and Banaji, 1994)

John Jost and Mahzarin Banaji proposed system justification theory (SJT) in 1994 to explain the paradox of outgroup favouritism — why members of disadvantaged groups sometimes hold negative stereotypes of their own group. The theory posits that people are motivated to "defend, bolster, and justify aspects of existing social, economic, and political systems," even when this serves against their objective interests, because doing so satisfies epistemic needs for order, existential needs for certainty, and relational needs for social harmony.

Jost's 2019 quarter-century review in the *British Journal of Social Psychology* (DOI: 10.1111/bjso.12297) confirmed:
- System justification "serves the palliative function of increasing satisfaction with the status quo."
- "The status-legitimacy hypothesis" predicts that members of low-status and disadvantaged groups are, under certain conditions, *more* likely than high-status members to perceive the system as legitimate — because the more one is disadvantaged by a system, the harsher the cognitive dissonance between experienced disadvantage and the motive to believe the system is fair.
- Economic system justification predicted *less* willingness to support disruptive protest (β = −.33, SE = .13, z = −2.54, p = .01) in study data summarised in Jost et al. ([Harvard DASH, "Effects of System Justification on Willingness to Protest," PDF](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/7312037d-7883-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/download)).
- System justification reduces group-based anger and thereby reduces both nondisruptive and disruptive collective protest ([Jost et al., 2017, "Missing in (Collective) Action," *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, DOI: 10.1177/0963721417690633](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417690633)).

Jost (2019) notes: "system-justification motivation can sometimes be harnessed to promote social change (Gaucher & Jost, 2011), and justice-based critiques may serve to delegitimise the status quo over an extended period of time" ([Jost, *British Journal of Social Psychology*, 2019](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjso.12297)).

**Key strategic implication.** SJT predicts that direct frontal attacks on existing structures — "the current system is unjust and must be overthrown" — will often trigger system justification *defensiveness*, particularly among the very people most disadvantaged by the system. Communication strategies that bypass this by starting with *existing shared values* (fairness, community, mutual support) and showing how the alternative *enacts those values more fully than the status quo does* are more likely to avoid triggering system-defensive responses.

The SSIR analysis of SJT for the medicines access movement identifies three practical strategies: (1) *expose contradictions* in the dominant narrative by posing "smart questions that get at contradictions" (these create cognitive dissonance that starts to "niggle" and erode the "naturalness" of the status quo); (2) *make alternatives visible and real* by giving them sensory, emotional, visceral power; (3) *deep canvassing* — extended conversations in which the person discovers inconsistencies in their own views — has shown particular promise ([SSIR, "System Justification Theory: What Makes Narrative Change So Hard?" 2022](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/what_makes_narrative_change_so_hard)).

### B3.4 — Gramsci, Hegemony, and the Battle for "Common Sense"

Antonio Gramsci, writing in his *Prison Notebooks* (1929–1935), developed the concept of **hegemony** to describe how ruling-class dominance is maintained not primarily through force but through cultural and ideological leadership — the creation of "common sense" (*senso comune*) values and assumptions that the population accepts as natural and self-evident. Common sense, for Gramsci, is "a largely uncritical and 'fragmentary' mode of consciousness" that "tends to invite resignation and passivity rather than collective action" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Antonio Gramsci"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/gramsci/)).

The strategic implication is that counter-hegemonic movements must win the **battle of ideas** — not just pass better policies, but reshape what people take for granted, what they regard as possible, what they regard as natural. Raymond Williams described hegemony as "a central system of practices, meanings and values saturating the consciousness of a society at a much deeper level than ordinary notions of ideology" — something that must be "continually renewed, recreated and defended" and is therefore also vulnerable to contestation ([Waging Nonviolence, "Lessons from Gramsci for Social Movements Today," 2023](https://wagingnonviolence.org/2023/08/gramsci-lessons-for-social-movements-today/)).

For social movements, Gramsci's framework implies:
1. Do not merely argue for better policies; challenge the underlying assumptions that make unjust arrangements appear normal.
2. Build "counter-hegemony" through cultural and intellectual work — stories, language, symbols — before political power can follow.
3. Organic intellectuals (those who emerge from and remain connected to their communities) are more effective at challenging hegemonic common sense than traditional intellectuals speaking from outside.
4. "War of position" — a long-term, multi-front contest for cultural and ideological influence — must precede "war of manoeuvre" (direct political confrontation).

A counter-hegemonic framing approach (CHFA) synthesising Gramsci and Goffman's frame theory has been developed by Hardnack (*Interface Journal*, 2019) to help movements engage in "the discursive work of frames which demystifies social relations and orients movements to contest hegemony" ([Hardnack, "Gramsci and Goffman, Together at Last," *Interface* 11(1), 2019](https://www.interfacejournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Interface-11-1-Hardnack.pdf)).

**Practical application.** Brehon Consensus can be positioned as making visible what the "common sense" of platform governance has rendered invisible: that communities are capable of self-governing, that this has been done throughout history, and that the assumption that you need a platform corporation or a technical expert to manage group decisions is an ideological imposition, not a natural law.

### B3.5 — The Backfire Effect and Psychological Reactance

An important concern for anyone communicating against a dominant narrative is whether corrective information triggers *reactance* — psychological resistance that actually strengthens existing beliefs. The "backfire effect" — the claim that corrections of factual misinformation can cause people to believe the misinformation *more strongly* — was popularised by Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler (2010). However, subsequent research has substantially revised this claim:

- Nyhan's own 2021 paper ("Why the Backfire Effect Does Not Explain the Durability of Political Misperceptions," *PNAS*, DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1912440117) argues that "an emerging research consensus finds that corrective information is typically at least somewhat effective at increasing belief accuracy when received by respondents." Backfire effects, while real in specific conditions of correction skepticism, are "not the primary challenge" for scientific communication ([Nyhan, *PNAS*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053951/)).
- A 2022 study of exposure to counter-attitudinal partisan news found "little evidence of boomerang effects" in an ecologically valid design tracking actual online exposure over time ([Casas et al., *Political Behavior*, 2022, DOI: 10.1007/s11109-021-09769-9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618323/)).
- A 2023 pre-registered study on standalone corrections found backfire only when participants were specifically induced to be skeptical of the correction — "as long as people are not skeptical of the correction, there is low risk that corrections targeting novel misinformation or reaching new audiences will backfire" ([Swire-Thompson et al., *Cognitive Research*, 2023, DOI: 10.1186/s41235-023-00492-z](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10317933/)).

**Psychological reactance** (Brehm, 1966) — the motivational state triggered when perceived freedom is threatened — remains a real concern, distinct from the backfire effect debate. Reactance is most likely when:
- The message is perceived as highly prescriptive or coercive ("you must do this").
- The audience perceives a threat to their autonomy or identity.
- The message comes from a distrusted source.

The implication for Brehon Consensus messaging is to frame adoption as a choice freely made by the community — not an external imposition, requirement, or correction of their current deficiencies. Language of invitation ("here's a tool some groups have found useful") is less likely to trigger reactance than language of prescription ("your current governance is broken and needs fixing").

## B4 — Loss-Aversion vs Hope/Agency Framing for Activating Adoption

### B4.1 — Prospect Theory and the Asymmetry of Gains and Losses

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect theory (1979; developed in "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk," *Econometrica* 47(2), 263–291) established that people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, and that losses loom larger than equivalent gains — loss aversion. The classic finding is that the pain of losing £100 is psychologically approximately twice as powerful as the pleasure of gaining £100. In the domain of risky choices, people tend to be risk-averse over gains (preferring a certain smaller gain to a gamble for a larger one) and risk-seeking over losses (preferring a gamble that might avoid a loss to accepting a certain smaller loss).

A 2024 *Nature Scientific Reports* study (Stecuła et al., DOI: 10.1038/s41598-024-61099-y) using a survey of 2,813 US adults tested prospect theory in the context of political risk (debt ceiling negotiations) and found evidence consistent with the original Kahneman-Tversky predictions: "most respondents are risk-averse to potential gains and risk-accepting to potential losses" ([Stecuła et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11076582/)).

---

### B4.2 — Gain vs Loss Framing Meta-Analyses: O'Keefe and Jensen

The most rigorous examination of gain vs loss framing for health behaviour change comes from Daniel O'Keefe's series of meta-analyses (University of Colorado / Northwestern), conducted with Jason Jensen:

- **Disease prevention behaviours** (O'Keefe & Jensen, 2007, *Journal of Health Communication* 12, 623–644, N = 21,656 across 93 studies): "gain-framed appeals, which emphasise the advantages of compliance with the communicator's recommendation, are statistically significantly more persuasive than loss-framed appeals... This difference is quite small (corresponding to r = .03)." Gain framing's advantage was largely attributable to dental hygiene; "the analysis finds no statistically significant differences in persuasiveness between gain- and loss-framed messages concerning other preventive actions such as safer-sex behaviors, skin cancer prevention behaviors, or diet and nutrition behaviors." ([PubMed summary, DOI: 10.1080/10810730701619695](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17934940/))
- **Disease detection behaviours** (O'Keefe & Jensen, 2009, *Journal of Communication* 59(2), 296–316, N = 9,145 across 53 studies): loss-framed appeals are "only slightly, but statistically significantly, more persuasive" for detection (r = −.039, p = .02), with the advantage largely confined to breast cancer detection. ([Journal of Communication, 2009](https://academic.oup.com/joc/article/59/2/296/4098529))
- **Physical activity** (O'Keefe meta-analysis): "gain-framed appeals are substantially more persuasive than loss-framed appeals." ([O'Keefe, University of Michigan presentation](https://www.bus.umich.edu/conferencefiles/public-health-communication/okeefe.pdf))

**Key practical conclusion.** The theoretical prediction from prospect theory that loss-framing should dominate persuasion has not been robustly confirmed in health communication meta-analyses. Effects are small overall (r ≈ .03–.04), context-dependent, and sometimes reversed. The "risk hypothesis" — that high-risk behaviours (detection) favour loss framing and low-risk behaviours (prevention) favour gain framing — is a widely cited heuristic but has itself been challenged in multiple studies ([van den Putte et al., *Psychology & Health*, 2014, DOI: 10.1080/08870446.2014.896916](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4349530/)).

A 2025 meta-analysis specifically on gain-loss framing in narrative persuasion (Journal of Communication, DOI published 2025, N = 16,361 across 47 studies) found that gain-framed narratives enhanced self-efficacy (d = 0.17, p = .009) and produced less counter-arguing (d = −0.57, p = .042) and more positive message evaluation (d = 0.25, p = .006). Loss-framed narratives produced greater transportation (d = −0.07, p = .039) and were more effective for donation behaviours and third-person narrative perspectives. ([Journal of Communication, 2025, DOI: jqaf004](https://academic.oup.com/joc/article/75/3/220/8011622?guestAccessKey=))

**For Brehon Consensus.** The evidence suggests that gain framing (what your community will gain: fairer decisions, equal voice, trust) is generally at least as effective as loss framing for adoption of new practices. Gain framing also produces higher self-efficacy — directly relevant to whether organisers believe *they* can actually do this. However, when communicating about existential risks to an existing community (a group fracturing due to opaque decision-making, power captured by a clique), loss framing may be appropriate as a *situation-diagnosis* component, provided it is paired with clear efficacy information about the solution.

---

### B4.3 — Hope vs Fear in Climate and Social Change Communication

The climate communication literature has grappled extensively with whether hope or fear activates pro-social behaviour. Key findings:

- Fear appeals can motivate action but also trigger maladaptive coping (denial, avoidance, apathy), especially when *not* paired with efficacy information. "Fear-inducing messages inhibit one's self-efficacy and mitigation behavioural intention" in several studies ([PMC, "When a Bleak Future Comes Closer," 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11577877/)).
- Hope is "strongly related to willingness to take pro-environmental actions and support climate change policies" and to "perceived climate change message effectiveness," but evidence also exists that certain types of hope — specifically "denial hope" (hope grounded in climate change not being a problem) and "status quo framing" — are associated with *less* engagement.
- A meta-analysis of 46 studies on hope and climate engagement (synthesised in [ouci.dntb.gov.ua, 2016](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/4KXkbWol/)) found: measured hope associated with greater climate engagement (r = 0.18 overall); hope regarding the possibility of *the respondent taking action* particularly strongly associated with greater engagement (r = 0.40); while hope that *climate change is not a problem* strongly negatively associated (r = −0.40). This typological distinction is critical.
- A 2025 study on hope vs fear in climate education found that "hope appeals highlighting actionable solutions strengthened collective efficacy by framing individual actions as steps toward systemic change. Fear appeals amplified focus on threats, exacerbating helplessness." ([Tandfonline, "Role of Emotional Messaging in Climate Education," 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00958964.2025.2558524))
- An experiment by Nabi, Gustafson, and Jensen (*Science Communication*, 2018) found "hope, as a key mediator between gain-framed messages and desired climate change policy attitudes and advocacy" — with gain-framed messages generating hope which in turn generated advocacy intention. ([Nabi et al., *Science Communication*, 2018, DOI: 10.1177/1075547018776019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1075547018776019))
- A study applying fear appeal theory to climate policy framing found that "a more threatening loss frame paired with an efficacy statement produced the strongest pro-policy attitudes and greatest willingness-to-pay by successfully balancing fear/threat with hope/efficacy" — concluding that neither pure gain framing nor pure loss framing is optimal; the combination of realistic threat acknowledgement *plus* credible efficacy information is most effective. ([Data.Mendeley, "Applying Fear Appeal Theory," 2018](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/8hmh5p6p3d/1))

**Practical synthesis.** The evidence strongly supports **efficacy-paired hope** as the primary activating frame for adoption of new practices by community organisers. The sequence that research supports is:
1. Acknowledge the real problem (the "hole" in the story spine — loss framing as situation diagnosis).
2. Pair it immediately with credible efficacy information: "here is what a group like yours did, and it worked."
3. Lead the resolution in gain/hope framing: "here is what you can achieve."

### B4.4 — Bandura's Self-Efficacy and Collective Efficacy

Albert Bandura's social cognitive theory (developed across works from 1977 to 2000) places *perceived self-efficacy* — "people's beliefs in their ability to produce given attainments" — as the most consistently powerful determinant of whether individuals initiate and sustain new behaviours. Efficacy beliefs affect "whether people think erratically or strategically, optimistically or pessimistically; what courses of action they choose to pursue; the goals they set for themselves and their commitment to them; how much effort they put forth in given endeavours; the outcomes they expect their efforts to produce; how long they persevere in the face of obstacles; their resilience to adversity" ([Bandura, 2000, "Exercise of Human Agency Through Collective Efficacy," *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, cited in scalar.usc.edu PDF](https://scalar.usc.edu/works/ismdia-590-community-data---s2019/media/Bandura2000.pdf)).

For community and collective action, Bandura extended the concept to **collective efficacy**: "People's shared beliefs in their collective power to produce desired results are a key ingredient of collective agency." Perceived collective efficacy "fosters groups' motivational commitment to their missions, resilience to adversity, and performance accomplishments." — it is an *emergent group-level property*, not simply the sum of individual self-efficacy beliefs. Bandura notes that "the higher the perceived collective political efficacy, the higher groups' motivational investment in their undertakings, the stronger their staying power in the face of impediments and setbacks, and the greater their performance accomplishments."

Research confirms that both self-efficacy (β = 0.078, SE = 0.036, p < .050) and mutual efficacy (β = 0.234, SE = 0.046, p < .001) are positive predictors of household activism in community settings ([Oxford Journals, *Social Work* 68(3), 2023](https://academic.oup.com/sw/article-abstract/68/3/192/7159710)). Mutual efficacy was more strongly associated with collective neighbourhood activism; self-efficacy with individual household activism.

**For Brehon Consensus.** Efficacy-building should be the primary communication goal. The single most effective thing the project can communicate is: *"a group just like yours did this, and it worked."* Demonstrated use cases, testimonials, and concrete success stories from similar organisations (mutual aid networks, worker co-ops, community unions) serve as vicarious efficacy models — Bandura's most powerful mechanism for building efficacy in others.

### B4.5 — Marshall Ganz: Public Narrative and Organising

Marshall Ganz — former United Farm Workers organiser and Harvard Kennedy School lecturer — developed the **Public Narrative** framework as a pedagogical and practical tool for social movement organising. It is now widely used in community organising training globally, including by the Obama 2008 campaign and across the civic society sector.

Public Narrative comprises three nested stories:

1. **Story of Self** — "Why am I called to lead?" Communicates the values that motivate the teller's leadership through specific *choice points*: moments of challenge, choice, and outcome. "Telling your Story of Self is a way to share the values that define who you are — not as abstract principles, but as lived experience." ([Ganz, "What Is Public Narrative?" 2008, PDF](https://changemakerspodcast.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ganz-WhatIsPublicNarrative08.pdf))

2. **Story of Us** — "Why are we called?" Expresses the shared values, shared experiences, and shared identity of the community addressed. The Story of Us invites listeners *into* an "us," making them part of a community of action.

3. **Story of Now** — "Why must we act now?" Articulates an urgent challenge to shared values that demands action, paints the "nightmare" if inaction continues, and the "dream" if the community acts together, ending with a concrete "hard ask."

Ganz writes: "Public narrative is a leadership practice of translating values into action. It is based on the fact that values are experienced emotionally. As such, they are sources of ends worthy of action and the capacity for action. Narrative is the discursive means we use to access values that equip us with the courage to make choices under conditions of uncertainty, to exercise agency." ([Ganz, "Public Narrative, Collective Action, and Power," in *Organizing for Social Change*, marshallganz.usmblogs.com PDF](http://marshallganz.usmblogs.com/files/2012/08/Public-Narrative-Collective-Action-and-Power.pdf))

The framework is explicitly designed to build the emotional resources needed for collective action in the face of uncertainty and potential failure — resources Ganz identifies as *hope, solidarity, and agency*. It is training in these emotional resources through story, not simply communication technique.

**Application to Brehon Consensus.** Organiser-trainers deploying Brehon Consensus in workshops or onboarding sessions should use the Public Narrative framework:
- **Story of Self**: Why does the facilitator care about fair governance? What injustice or fracture have they witnessed?
- **Story of Us**: What shared values unite this community? What have they already overcome together?
- **Story of Now**: What decision or conflict is before them right now that Brehon Consensus could help resolve fairly? What is at stake if they don't get it right? What is the one action they can take today?

This positions the governance tool not as a software product to be adopted but as a practice of self-governance that enacts the community's already-held values.

## Key Unverifiable or Contested Claims Flagged

- ⚠ **Identifiable victim effect** as originally described (individual > statistics for donations) has failed pre-registered replication. The broader principle that concrete individuals are more emotionally engaging than abstract statistics remains supported by narrative transportation research, but the specific directional claim is contested.
- ⚠ **Lakoff's neural framing claims** (frames as physical neural circuits that are changed by language repetition) are contested by cognitive scientists as reductionist and not empirically demonstrated to the degree Lakoff implies. The practical communication prescriptions are better supported than the underlying neuroscientific account.
- ⚠ **Common Cause intrinsic/extrinsic value distinction** conflates two distinct theoretical models (Schwartz's circumplex and SDT's continuum), and while the directional evidence is consistently in favour of intrinsic framing, individual experimental effect sizes are often small or non-significant in direct replication.
- ⚠ **Backfire effect** in the strong sense (corrections cause stronger false belief) is not reliably reproduced outside very specific conditions of correction-skepticism and does not appear to be a major practical obstacle for most communications contexts.

## C1. Building Credibility With Sceptical, Savvy Audiences

### The Hovland Model: Expertise vs. Trustworthiness

The foundational framework for understanding source credibility comes from Carl Hovland's post-war programme at Yale. [Hovland & Weiss (1951)](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/HovlandWeiss-POQ-1951.pdf) demonstrated experimentally that *opinions change significantly more* when a message comes from a high-credibility source, and that source credibility comprises two orthogonal components: **expertise** (does the communicator know what they are talking about?) and **trustworthiness** (are they being honest?). Crucially, the two components can pull in opposite directions: a source that is highly expert but appears to have a vested interest suffers on trustworthiness even if their factual claims are sound. As the [Wikipedia summary of source credibility theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_credibility) notes, Hovland's Yale group confirmed that "the credibility of all communication, regardless of format, has been found to be heavily influenced by the *perceived* credibility of the source."

Later research sharpened this distinction. Susmann & Wegener (2022) in *Memory & Cognition* found that source expertise independently improves retraction believability — but **only when the source has no vested interest** in issuing it; a solo developer with nothing financially at stake from their community's adoption of their own tool has a genuine trustworthiness advantage over a commercial vendor ([PMC9718466](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9718466/)). The practical implication: a new project cannot claim expertise by assertion, but it *can* signal trustworthiness by behaving in ways that are costly to fake — publishing limitations honestly, opening the codebase, and acknowledging what is not yet built.

**For Brehon Consensus:** The absence of a corporate sponsor, while a liability for expertise signals, is a potential asset for trustworthiness. It should be foregrounded, not apologised for.

---

### The Pratfall Effect (Aronson, 1966): Admitting Flaws Increases Likeability

One of the most counterintuitive and robustly replicated findings in social psychology is that admitting imperfections *increases* perceived attractiveness and trustworthiness for sources already perceived as competent. Elliot Aronson's 1966 experiment, conducted at the University of Minnesota, had subjects listen to a recording of a highly competent contestant (92% correct answers on a quiz) and a mediocre one (30%). When the highly competent performer accidentally spilled coffee on himself, listeners rated him *more* attractive than the unspilled, perfect version. The mediocre performer who spilt coffee was rated *less* attractive. The effect, known as the [Pratfall Effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratfall_effect), has since been replicated and extended to brands and organisations.

> "Originally described in 1966 by Elliot Aronson, the pratfall effect is the tendency for a person's likability to change after making a mistake depending on perceived competence: highly competent individuals tend to become more likable, while average individuals may become less likable for the same error." — [Wikipedia, Pratfall Effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratfall_effect)

The mechanism is one of *humanisation*: the flaw signals that the source is not strategic or polished in the way that PR-coached actors are, which raises the perceived authenticity of everything else they say. A [World Economic Forum analysis](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2018/02/why-admitting-a-weakness-makes-people-like-you-more/) of Aronson's work summarises: "When a capable person makes a blunder, it becomes more relatable — and likability rises."

**For Brehon Consensus:** This is a direct argument for the "alpha, solo-built, work in progress" framing — not as a disclaimer, but as a positive signal. Upfront acknowledgements such as "this is unfinished and here is exactly what it cannot yet do" should not be buried in small print; they should be prominent, because a savvy, sceptical audience will search for the catch and if they find it themselves, the effect is lost. Volunteer the catch first.

### "Show Your Working": Transparency as Trust Production

Jay Rosen's influential 2017 essay [*Show Your Work: The New Terms for Trust in Journalism*](https://pressthink.org/2017/12/show-work-new-terms-trust-journalism/) crystallises an emerging norm that applies far beyond journalism: "Transparency is the new objectivity." The argument — derived from David Weinberger and the *Elements of Journalism* tradition — is that a sceptical, networked public can no longer be expected to extend *ex ante* credibility to any institution. Trust must be *earned through verifiability*, not assumed from credentials. Rosen identifies eleven practices of transparency that build trust: disclosing where you are coming from, stating what you do not know, inviting correction, attributing every claim to a source, and showing the reasoning behind decisions.

The [American Press Institute's guidance on transparency and credibility](https://americanpressinstitute.org/transparency-credibility/) develops this further, arguing that transparency signals function as costly commitments — they are hard to fake because they invite scrutiny. Practitioners in the field of risk communication have codified this as the "inform, not persuade" norm: the Winton Centre for Risk and Evidence Communication at Cambridge formulated [five rules for evidence communication](https://plus.maths.org/content/be-clear-be-trustworthy): "Inform, not persuade; Balance, but not false balance; Disclose uncertainty; Acknowledge complexity; Be transparent about methods." The [FT Strategies piece on audience trust](https://www.ftstrategies.com/en-gb/insights/part-3-creating-audience-trust-by-showing-your-work) calls this approach "making trust your core value proposition."

A [civic-communication strategist writing on Substack](https://simonbaugh.substack.com/p/winning-strategies-pt-4-demonstrate?action=share) adds: "Transparency is judged to be more trustworthy by sceptics. Signals of honesty include: admitting uncertainty or mistakes; providing information that is balanced and nuanced, including presenting evidence that runs counter to your own argument."

**For Brehon Consensus:** The architecture for showing your working already exists: a public git repository, an honest changelog, an openly-maintained design doc, a public issue tracker with real bugs listed, and a `LIMITATIONS.md` file. Each of these is a trust signal that costs nothing but seriousness.

---

### Social Proof (Cialdini) for New Projects Without a Track Record

Robert Cialdini's foundational work on influence, first published in 1984, identifies social proof as one of the six (later seven) primary mechanisms of persuasion: "In uncertainty, people look at what others do" ([Sue Behavioural Design summary of Cialdini's principles](https://www.suebehaviouraldesign.com/en/blog/cialdini-principles-of-persuasion/)). For organisations with an established user base, this is straightforward. For a new, alpha-stage project it presents an obvious problem.

Cialdini's own answer — as outlined in [a recent post on his LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/robertcialdini_socialproof-influence-persuasion-activity-7328811456710303745-h0dC) — is "future social proof": communicating what respected peers *intend to do* or what early adopters have *said*, rather than numerical adoption. For an activist/grassroots project, the relevant social proof is *peer endorsement* from trusted figures within target communities (known organisers, respected open-source contributors, digital rights advocates) rather than download counts or media coverage.

The [Nielsen Norman Group's UX research on social proof](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/social-proof-ux/) notes that the most persuasive social proof comes from sources the audience identifies as *similar to themselves*, not from large numbers of strangers. For a project aimed at non-technical grassroots organisers, the right social proof is a named grassroots organiser saying why they use it — not aggregate star counts.

---

### Open-Source Trust Signals

Academic research on trust in open-source software identifies a set of visible, behaviour-based signals that influence credibility judgements. A think-aloud study on prototypical trust signals ([Cranfield University / dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk](https://dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk/items/530c41e3-57de-4ef2-985e-224e43fbbf9d)) found that users relied on a hierarchy of direct and indirect signals including: active issue responses, semver-consistent releases, clear licensing, presence of tests, and maintainer responsiveness. A 2024 Carnegie Mellon paper on [decomposing and measuring trust in OSS](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icsenier24.pdf) formalised these into behaviour-based indicators: CI pipeline security checks, repository badges, time from first PR to commit access, and maintainer tenure.

A Hacker News thread on [Trust Signals in Open Source Projects](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=32174301) — a reliable proxy for savvy technical community attitudes — listed the most influential trust signals as: maintenance frequency, maintainer engagement with issues, adherence to semver, and transparency about dependencies and failures. Several commenters noted that *absence of social proof* is not necessarily a red flag provided the above signals are present: "I care about whether it's maintained and how open the author is to external ideas."

The [Linux Foundation's guidance on improving trust in OSS](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/hubfs/Reports/improving_trust_security_in_oss_projects.pdf) recommends publishing a project-level `SECURITY.md`, disclosing known vulnerabilities, and having a transparent governance document — all low-cost, high-credibility-signal actions for a solo developer.

**For Brehon Consensus:** An alpha-stage solo project with an honest `README`, a visible changelog, a public issue tracker, a named human contact, and an explicit statement of what the project is *not trying to do* is better positioned with a savvy audience than a polished website with vague claims about "revolutionary" features.

## C2. How Over-Claiming and Hype Damage Trust

### The Persuasion Knowledge Model (Friestad & Wright, 1994)

The most influential theoretical account of why audiences resist marketing language comes from Marian Friestad and Peter Wright's [Persuasion Knowledge Model](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/21/1/1/1853712), published in *Journal of Consumer Research* (Volume 21, Issue 1, 1994). The PKM argues that people develop over time a lay theory of how persuasion works, which they use to *cope with* persuasion attempts. When an audience detects that they are being persuaded — through recognition of persuasion tactics such as exaggerated claims, urgency framing, or buzzword use — they activate their persuasion knowledge, which typically produces one of three responses: scepticism, reactance (negative rebound), or inertia.

Crucially, the PKM predicts that *sophistication of the audience increases the backfire risk*. A dataset paper from the 2024 LREC Conference ([aclanthology.org](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.1295.pdf)) summarises: "According to the Persuasion Knowledge Model (Friestad & Wright, 1994), recognising a persuasion attempt changes the target's perceptions of the agent's intentions and, consequently, influences their responses. Since people like preserving their freedom and autonomy, they will likely resist persuasion threatening their autonomy." For anti-corporate and activist audiences, who have above-average persuasion literacy, the activation threshold is lower.

Research in *Journal of Consumer Research* extending the PKM found that accessing persuasion knowledge can actually *bolster* critical thinking, causing people not just to discount the message but to scrutinise the messenger — making *any* subsequent claim harder to accept ([Beyond Skepticism: Can Accessing Persuasion Knowledge Bolster Critical Thinking?](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/43/6/895/2687771)). Once a project has triggered persuasion knowledge activation, it is very difficult to recover trust within the same interaction.

**For Brehon Consensus:** Any language that pattern-matches to marketing — "revolutionary," "game-changing," "the only solution," "trust in the digital age" — will trigger PKM activation in exactly the audience the project most needs to reach. This is not a soft preference; it is a structural feature of how sophisticated audiences process persuasion.

---

### Expectation Disconfirmation: When Hype Creates Its Own Failure Mode

A distinct mechanism through which over-claiming causes trust damage is expectation disconfirmation. Oliver's (1980) expectation-disconfirmation theory, originally developed for consumer satisfaction, holds that satisfaction is a function of the gap between prior expectations and actual experience. When expectations are inflated by marketing claims, even genuinely adequate products produce *negative* disconfirmation. Research specifically examining trust disconfirmation in technology contexts ([Incorporating trust-in-technology into Expectation Disconfirmation Theory](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963868713000607); *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*, 2013) found that when users' hands-on experience fails to match trust expectations set during discovery, satisfaction and continuation intent drop sharply — and these effects are *worse than if no expectations had been set at all*.

A 2015 paper in *Information Systems Research* ([Using Expectation Disconfirmation Theory and Trust-in-Technology](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/isre.2015.0611)) found specifically that negative trust disconfirmation — where a product was claimed to be more trustworthy than it is — produced especially durable damage to trusting intention, because trust violation activates attributional reasoning about motive ("why would they tell me this if it wasn't true?").

An academic analysis of misleading product claims in *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* ([Great expectations and broken promises](https://go.gale.com/ps/i.do?aty=open-web-entry&id=GALE%7CA360610740&issn=00920703&it=r&linkaccess=abs&p=AONE&sid=googleScholar&sw=w&userGroupName=anon~5e7237bd&v=2.1)) identified *distrust* — not mere dissatisfaction — as the consequence of negative expectancy disconfirmation after misleading claims: "Misleading claims result in distrust as an additional byproduct of negative expectancy disconfirmation."

From the practitioner side, a marketing analysis by [Fusion Marketing Services](https://www.fusion-marketing-services.co.uk/news/why-marketing-hype-is-damaging) documents the mechanism clearly: "The overuse of superlatives, hyperbole, and exaggerated claims *damages the trust, credibility, and ultimately the sales of the brands using it*. Consumer scepticism increases with overclaiming."

---

### Hype and Public Trust in Science and Technology

Master & Resnik's peer-reviewed paper [Hype and Public Trust in Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3954634/) (*Science and Engineering Ethics*, 2013) provides the strongest academic articulation of the causal mechanism:

> "Many scholars claim that hyping biotechnology results in a loss of public trust, and possibly public enthusiasm or support for science, because public expectations of the biotechnological promises will be unmet."

The paper notes that the mechanism works through a two-stage process: hype inflates public expectations, and when those expectations are not met, the gap is attributed not to complexity or difficulty but to dishonesty or incompetence on the part of those who communicated the hype. The Cambridge *Canadian Journal of Philosophy* paper [Understanding the Problem of "Hype"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-philosophy/article/understanding-the-problem-of-hype-exaggeration-values-and-trust-in-science/E17D9D19E73E9E5BD748A0A022574492) adds that hype exaggerates benefits while understating risks, and that when scientists communicate hype, "public trust in science is undermined as well as enthusiasm for particular technologies."

---

### Marketing-Speak and Buzzwords: The Credibility Cost

The persuasion knowledge literature is supported by practitioner evidence that jargon and buzzwords actively erode credibility. An [Agency Analytics analysis](https://agencyanalytics.com/blog/marketing-buzzwords) documents that overused terms ("omnichannel synergy," "growth hacking," "AI-powered") "have become a shortcut — a way to *sound* insightful without actually saying anything" and "quietly erode clarity and trust." The [Brand Blueprint analysis](https://www.brandblueprint.ai/blog/stop-using-buzzwords-and-jargon-in-your-marketing) finds that "plain language marketing consistently outperforms jargon-heavy messaging because it connects directly with how people think and make decisions."

For anti-corporate and activist audiences, the issue is not merely aesthetic. Corporate buzzwords are a *genre signal*: they indicate that the communicator has been trained in, or has absorbed the habits of, PR and marketing. For audiences who are explicitly anti-corporate, recognising this genre is itself a trust-disqualifying move. A [Brand Equity / Economic Times analysis](https://brandequity.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/marketing/buzzwords-instead-of-real-outcomes-the-recipe-for-mistrust/121426808) puts it plainly: "Buzzwords instead of real outcomes: the recipe for mistrust."

**For Brehon Consensus:** The relevant vocabulary regime is that of technical documentation and activist organising — precise, honest, self-deprecating where appropriate, and free of terms that signal commercial intent. Phrases like "trust layer," "governance infrastructure," and "community consensus protocol" are borderline; they should be tested against whether a sceptical reader would read them as marketing-speak.

## C3. How to Talk About Reputation Without Evoking Surveillance

### The Social-Credit Misread: Why the Framing Risk Is Real

The Western imaginary around digitised reputation scoring is dominated by two reference points: the [Black Mirror "Nosedive" episode (2016)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nosedive_(Black_Mirror)) — a satirical dystopia in which every social interaction is rated 1–5 stars by smartphone and these ratings determine access to housing, travel, and employment — and the Chinese Social Credit System (SCS). The *Nosedive* episode is set in a pastel-coloured suburban America, deliberately designed to make the dystopia recognisably Western and domestically achievable, rather than exotic or foreign. Its 8.5/10 IMDb rating and status as the third-highest-rated *Black Mirror* episode suggests it landed as a credible near-future scenario for large audiences.

Research on Western perceptions of the Chinese SCS demonstrates that the social-credit framing is already embedded in discourse. A 2023 paper in *New Media & Society* ([Media framing and public support for China's social credit system](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/14614448231187823); Xu, Krueger et al., University of Rhode Island) found that "although China's Social Credit System is widely portrayed by Western media as repressive surveillance, recent studies show that it receives high levels of support among Chinese citizens" — a finding that illustrates the power of media *framing* over the underlying mechanics of any reputational system. A 2025 *Information, Communication & Society* paper ([Techno-orientalism in the US media: the case of China's social credit system](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369118X.2025.2565335)) notes that the imagined techno-dystopian version of the SCS — "an Orwellian scoring scheme through which the Communist Party controls citizens' every step" — "proliferates in Western media, despite scholarship pointing out that it has little in common with the realities of SCSs in China."

The implication for Brehon Consensus is that any system that can be described as "scoring users" in a summary phrase will be mapped, by a non-trivial proportion of the target audience, onto either Nosedive or the SCS misread. This is not a fringe concern; it is the default mental model that is activated when people encounter an unfamiliar reputation system.

---

### The Prosocial Framing: Contribution-Earned Standing

The best evidence for an alternative framing comes from the biology of cooperation. Nowak & Sigmund's landmark review [Evolution of Indirect Reciprocity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16251955/) (*Nature*, 2005) is the foundational scientific account of why reputation systems exist at all:

> "How can natural selection promote unselfish behaviour? Various mechanisms have been proposed, and a rich analysis of indirect reciprocity has recently emerged: I help you and somebody else helps me. The evolution of cooperation by indirect reciprocity leads to reputation building, morality judgement and complex social interactions with ever-increasing cognitive demands."

The key theoretical point is that reputation, in the evolutionary account, is not a surveillance tool — it is a *signal of prosocial behaviour* that enables strangers to cooperate without direct prior experience of each other. The reputation score in Nowak & Sigmund's model is not assigned by authorities; it accrues through observed acts of helping. A player who helps others in public gains an "image score" that makes others more willing to help them in turn.

This theoretical foundation maps directly onto the distinction between **contribution-earned standing** and **compliance-monitored scoring**:

| Dimension | Contribution-Earned Standing | Compliance/Surveillance Scoring |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin of score** | Actions taken to help others | Avoidance of penalised behaviours |
| **Agency** | Active, voluntary | Passive, monitored |
| **Who judges** | Community peers | System authority |
| **Primary emotion** | Pride, mutual recognition | Fear of deduction |
| **Black Mirror / SCS risk** | Low | High |

Empirical research on prosocial reputation confirms this framing is not merely rhetorical. A 2013 study ([Cooperative behaviour and prosocial reputation dynamics in a Caribbean village](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3652444/)) tracked prosocial reputation in a real-world community over 20 months and found that "prosocial reputations correlate with the number of cooperative acts one performs" — earned through doing, not through compliance. [Scarce and directly beneficial reputations support cooperation](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-68123-x) (*Scientific Reports*, 2020) found that reputational information that flows from behaviour — rather than being assigned — is more credible and more effective at sustaining cooperation.

The Royal Society *Philosophical Transactions B* paper [The psychological foundations of reputation-based cooperation](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.2020.0287) adds: "Humans care about having a positive reputation, which may prompt them to help in scenarios where the return benefits are not obvious. Various game-theoretical models support the hypothesis that concern for reputation may stabilise cooperation beyond kin, pairs or small groups."

---

### HCI / CSCW Research on Reputation System Design

The computer-supported cooperative work literature has extensively studied how reputation systems function in real online communities and what design choices affect user trust.

Paul Resnick, Richard Zeckhauser et al.'s canonical paper [Reputation Systems: Facilitating Trust in Internet Interactions](http://presnick.people.si.umich.edu/papers/cacm00/reputations.pdf) (*Communications of the ACM*, 2000) defines the three requirements for a functional reputation system: entities must be long-lived (so future interaction is expected), feedback on current interactions must be captured and distributed, and past feedback must guide future decisions. The paper frames the purpose of reputation systems as essentially prosocial and cooperative: "these systems help people decide whom to trust, encourage trustworthy behaviour, and deter participation by those who are unskilled or dishonest." Critically, Resnick et al. frame reputation as a mechanism for "restoring the shadow of the future to each transaction" — making isolated interactions feel like part of a long-term relationship. This is the opposite of surveillance; it is the infrastructure of community memory.

Adler, de Alfaro et al.'s [Reputation Systems for Open Collaboration](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3615714/) (*Communications of the ACM*, 2011) studied Wikipedia and the Yelp-precursor platform, finding that **content-driven reputation systems** — where users gain or lose standing based on how their contributions are received by peers — are qualitatively different from engagement-metric systems. In content-driven systems, "authors of long-lived contributions gain reputation, while authors of reverted contributions lose reputation." This creates alignment between the score and genuine value to the community, not between the score and social performance.

A [Stanford CS course analysis of online community reputation systems](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/eroberts/courses/cs181/projects/2010-11/PsychologyOfTrust/rep3.html) contrasts Reddit karma, Hacker News karma, and Stack Overflow reputation, noting the design differences: Stack Overflow's unlocking of progressively greater community governance powers as reputation grows creates "alignment between the score and genuine value to the community" — you earn the right to do more, not just a number on your profile. This stands in sharp contrast to social media engagement metrics (likes, follower counts), which measure social performance, not contribution.

The [Carnegie Mellon analysis of Stack Overflow's reputation system](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ymovshov/Papers/asonam_2013.pdf) found that "participation in activities on the site (such as asking and answering questions) earns users reputation, which is an indicator of the value of that user to the site" — and that "very high reputation users are the primary source of answers, and especially of high quality answers." The reputation score, in other words, does what it is supposed to: it reliably identifies people who have contributed quality help.

---

### Distinguishing Contribution-Based Standing from Engagement/Popularity Metrics

A critical framing distinction — directly relevant to the "social-credit misread" risk — is between reputation earned through **contribution** and reputation as a measure of **engagement or popularity**. Likes, follower counts, and upvote totals measure social *performance* — how well someone optimises for attention. They are gameable, they reward social conformity, and they are structurally similar to the Nosedive scenario, where ratings reflect how well you make others feel in a fleeting interaction.

Contribution-based standing, by contrast, measures **value delivered to others**: answers that solved problems, governance decisions that were followed, code that was merged, documentation that was used. It is harder to game because it requires doing the actual work.

Slashdot's karma system (1997–present) was one of the first explicit attempts at this: as [Slashdot's FAQ](https://slashdot.org/faq/karma.shtml) describes, karma represents "how your comments have been moderated" on scales of "interesting," "insightful," and "informative" — categories that require peer judgement of substantive content, not merely social approval.

A [New Public report on Reputation Management Systems](https://newpublic.org/uploads/2024/10/Case-Study_Reputation-Management-Systems.pdf) explicitly distinguishes reputation systems built on "earned badges, qualifiers on a profile, visual logos or insignia, or stated certifications" that allow users to differentiate themselves through contribution, from social media systems where "follower counts" proxy popularity. The Consortium for Service Innovation's [Reputation Models & Badging framework](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/Intelligent_Swarming/Practices_Guide/50_Intelligent_Swarming_Practices/20_Recognize/15_Reputation_Models_Badging) states: "Reputation represents the history of contribution (value) a person has created with their skills and abilities, *as seen by the people receiving the value*."

---

### Practical Framing Guidance

Based on the above, the social-credit misread is most likely to be triggered when a reputation system is described using language that implies:
- scoring of *behaviour* rather than *contributions*
- assignment of scores *by the system* rather than by peers
- *consequences* for low scores (reduced access, penalties)
- scores that are *persistent and visible* to third parties outside the community

The framing that defuses the misread emphasises:
- **Agency**: "you build standing by doing things that help others"
- **Community judgment**: "your peers recognise what you've contributed"
- **Non-punitive**: "a new participant starts with no standing, not negative standing"
- **Contribution, not compliance**: "standing reflects what you've given, not what you've avoided"

The distinction between "you earn standing by helping others" and "your score reflects your compliance with community norms" is not merely semantic — it maps onto a genuine design and architectural choice, and communicating it accurately signals a genuine design philosophy.

## C4. Avoiding Crypto/Blockchain Association Damage

### Why This Risk Is Acute for Civic-Tech Projects

The FOSS and activist communities that Brehon Consensus targets have, since approximately 2017–2021, developed strongly negative priors toward blockchain and cryptocurrency-adjacent projects. This is not irrational: the period produced an enormous volume of projects that co-opted open-source and decentralisation language for speculative financial instruments. The result is that **any project using decentralisation, consensus, or governance language** is at risk of being pattern-matched to crypto, regardless of whether it has any blockchain component.

What follows is a catalogue of documented cases of how crypto association caused trust damage to projects with genuine community credibility.

---

### Case 1: Signal / MobileCoin (2021)

Signal, the gold-standard privacy-focused messaging application, integrated a cryptocurrency called MobileCoin into its UK beta in April 2021. The backlash from Signal's own community was immediate and intense.

[CoinTelegraph's reporting](https://cointelegraph.com/news/revealed-top-4-user-complaints-about-signal-s-new-crypto-payments-beta) documented that a major category of user complaints — beyond practical concerns about fees and liquidity — was the simple objection that "cryptocurrency is the worst." The community's objections were structural, not marginal: privacy researchers and security experts noted that MobileCoin's founder Moxie Marlinspike appeared on an early draft of MobileCoin's white paper as its CTO; that MobileCoin was 100% premined (all tokens created at genesis by the founders); and that the MOB token price surged from $7 to $65 in the days before the Signal integration announcement, raising obvious questions about insider trading. Security researcher Nicholas Weaver summarised the community view on Twitter: "Translation: Signal isn't integrating just any random shitcoin, it is integrating Moxie Marlinspike's random shitcoin. Gee, what a coinkidink."

The case is instructive because Signal had among the highest possible pre-existing credibility in the privacy and activist community. The MobileCoin integration did not destroy Signal as a product — it remains widely used — but it permanently damaged trust for a significant portion of its audience and became a cautionary reference case in FOSS/privacy community discussions. [Reddit's r/privacytoolsIO thread on the integration](https://www.reddit.com/r/privacytoolsIO/comments/ndiv6x/will_signal_app_be_safe_after_the_implementation/) captures the community response: the integration was experienced as a betrayal of Signal's mission, regardless of whether the crypto feature was technically optional.

**Key lesson:** Even a technically optional crypto feature, when associated with a founding-team financial interest, is read as a betrayal of the trust relationship, not a neutral product addition.

---

### Case 2: Mozilla / Firefox (2022)

In early January 2022, the Mozilla Foundation invited netizens to donate via cryptocurrency, using a new payment service provider. The backlash was swift and came from within Mozilla's own founding community. [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2022/01/06/mozilla-foundation-crypto/) that Jamie Zawinski — a co-founder of the Mozilla Project — wrote: "Everyone involved in the project should be witheringly ashamed of this decision to partner with planet-incinerating Ponzi grifters." Peter Linss, founder of the Gecko browser engine, wrote that Mozilla was "meant to be better than this."

Mozilla paused its crypto donation acceptance within days, pledging to review whether it "fits with our climate goals." The episode illustrated that even *passive acceptance* of cryptocurrency (as a donation method, not a product feature) was read by the FOSS and open-web community as a values misalignment — not as financial pragmatism.

[TechRepublic's reporting](https://techcrunch.com/2022/01/06/mozilla-foundation-crypto/) noted that the Mozilla backlash was explicitly cited as motivation and inspiration for subsequent community action at Wikimedia. The causal chain — Mozilla crypto → Wikimedia community discussion → Wikimedia vote to ban crypto donations — demonstrates the *contagion effect*: once an open-source community project accepts or integrates crypto, it triggers active consideration by peer communities of their own position.

---

### Case 3: Wikimedia / Wikipedia (2022)

In April 2022, the Wikimedia Foundation community voted 71% in favour of ending all cryptocurrency donation acceptance, following a proposal by editor Molly White (operator of the web3isgoinggreat.com documentation site). [Gizmodo's report](https://gizmodo.com/wikipedia-will-no-longer-accept-cryptocurrency-donation-1848867154) quotes White directly: "There are just too many issues with crypto for any potential donation revenue to be worth the cost of *helping to legitimise it*."

The vote outcome — 232 to 94 — was striking given that cryptocurrency had accounted for just 0.08% of Wikimedia's donations in 2021 (approximately $130,000). The community was willing to forgo a small revenue stream specifically to avoid reputational association with cryptocurrency. Lisa Seitz-Gruwell, Wikimedia's chief advancement officer, confirmed the closure of the BitPay account entirely.

**Key lesson:** The cost-benefit calculation for Wikimedia's community was not primarily financial — it was reputational. Even a financially trivial association with crypto was perceived as worth actively severing.

---

### Case 4: SourceHut / Drew DeVault (2022–2023)

Drew DeVault, the founder of SourceHut (a FOSS code hosting service), announced in October 2022 that SourceHut would ban all cryptocurrency and blockchain-related projects from its platform, citing them as primarily used for "get-rich-quick schemes" and criminal activity. [TechRepublic's report](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/open-source-repository-sourcehut-removes-all-cryptocurrency-projects/) quotes DeVault:

> "Few to no legitimate use-cases for this technology have been found; instead it is mostly used for fraudulent 'get-rich-quick' schemes and to facilitate criminal activity, such as ransomware, illicit trade and sanctions evasion. These projects often encourage large-scale energy waste and electronics waste... The presence of these projects on SourceHut exposes new victims to these scams and is harmful to the reputation of SourceHut and its community."

DeVault had previously written an influential post titled [Cryptocurrency is an Abject Disaster](https://drewdevault.com/blog/Cryptocurrency-is-a-disaster/) (April 2021), which documented that CI platforms had collectively wasted "thousands of hours of engineering time" dealing with abuse of free CPU resources for cryptocurrency mining. This was a concrete, operational grievance, not merely an ideological one.

The SourceHut case is significant because DeVault explicitly framed the ban in terms of *community protection* and *reputational integrity*: hosting crypto projects damaged SourceHut's relationship with its target community. This is the same logic that will operate for Brehon Consensus.

---

### Case 5: Mastodon and the Fediverse Explicitly Distance From Crypto

The Mastodon project and the broader Fediverse community have made their disassociation from blockchain and Web3 explicit and proactive. [Fedi.tips, a widely-cited unofficial Fediverse guide](https://fedi.tips/does-mastodon-or-the-fediverse-use-ads-or-trackers-or-algorithms-or-blockchain-or-cryptocurrency-or-anything-annoying-like-that/), states directly:

> "No. There are no ads, no trackers, the timeline shows all posts from everyone you follow in chronological order, and there is no blockchain/cryptocurrency/web3. Fediverse servers connect to each other using traditional sustainable methods that email and websites have used for decades... The so-called 'web3' is just marketing crap that con artists have used to promote blockchain-based get-rich-quick schemes. Part of the deceptive marketing around web3 scams includes trying to steal the term 'decentralised', but in reality blockchain and cryptocurrency schemes are just about trying to make money through dodgy investments."

This framing — *decentralisation is an older, proven, non-crypto idea* — is exactly the pre-emptive disassociation strategy that civic-tech projects in the same space need to adopt. Mastodon's decentralisation credibility is partly *built on* its explicit non-crypto identity.

[A Hacker News thread on the relationship between Mastodon and Web3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=33621498) captured the community norm: "Mastodon is not web3, in fact doesn't want to be associated with the term 'web3' at all." The Mastodon project signed the European Social Stack Open Declaration in 2025 to further anchor its identity in federation/open protocols rather than crypto/Web3.

---

### Case 6: Brave Browser / BAT Token

Brave's integration of the Basic Attention Token (BAT) into its browser is a case study in how even a well-intentioned privacy-aligned browser can be perceived as compromised by its crypto integration. The Brave community forum has multiple threads documenting user perceptions of [breach of trust](https://community.brave.app/t/breach-of-trust-by-brave/462965), and a 2020 incident in which Brave was caught [automatically inserting affiliate links](https://www.enterprisetimes.co.uk/2020/06/09/brave-browser-accused-of-trust-breach/) into user searches — directly contradicting its privacy promises — accelerated community disillusionment. [Reddit's r/CryptoCurrency discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/brave_browser/comments/vyjufp/is_it_me_or_has_the_enthusiasm_for_bat_token_and/) observed that "BAT has the problem a lot of other cryptocurrencies have: if the discussion and activity around the token is more about its value than its implementation and practical use, it's probably dead in the water."

The Brave case demonstrates that even a genuine privacy product can have its credibility hollowed out by crypto association, because the crypto discussion *crowds out* the privacy discussion and attracts a different community with different values.

---

### FOSS Community Hostility: Structural, Not Incidental

The above cases are not isolated incidents; they reflect a structural community norm. DeVault's 2021 blog post is a canonical expression of FOSS community sentiment. [A Hacker News thread on crypto grifters recruiting open-source AI developers](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46654878) documents active patterns of "recruiting" open-source project maintainers into crypto schemes — and the community's active resistance to such recruitment. The thread captures the norm: genuine FOSS contributors do not consider crypto-associated projects as peers; they consider them parasites on FOSS infrastructure and terminology.

A Placeholder VC analysis ([FOSS Governance and Blockchain Networks](https://www.placeholder.vc/blog/2020/7/14/foss-governance-and-blockchain-networks)) acknowledges the tension: "The principles of free and open source software are fundamental to the ethos of the communities building blockchain networks. While it is not uncommon for a single organization or small group of core developers to coordinate most of the work, the source code is generally open..." — but the community perception is that blockchain projects borrow FOSS aesthetics without FOSS ethics.

---

### Pre-Emptive Disassociation Strategy for Brehon Consensus

Based on the evidence above, the following communication choices are indicated:

1. **Name the association explicitly and deny it directly.** Waiting for users to raise the crypto question is a losing strategy — they will raise it, and if there is no prepared answer, the absence reads as confirmation. Fedi.tips' approach of directly stating "there is no blockchain/cryptocurrency/web3" in response to an anticipated question is the model.

2. **Frame decentralisation in pre-crypto historical terms.** Email, the telephone network, and the postal service are federated (decentralised) systems that predate cryptocurrency by decades. Anchoring Brehon Consensus's governance model in this older, proven tradition — not in Web3 or crypto decentralisation discourse — makes the disassociation credible rather than merely defensive.

3. **Distinguish "consensus" (the mechanism) from "consensus" (the blockchain term).** The word "consensus" has been colonised by blockchain discourse. Brehon Consensus should directly address this: "Consensus here refers to the centuries-old meaning — agreement among community members — not the blockchain proof-of-consensus mechanism."

4. **Proactively state that the project has no token, no cryptocurrency component, and no plans to add one.** This is the SourceHut approach: make the disassociation a positive commitment, not just an absence.

## C2.1 'Cancel Culture' as a Politically Charged, Contested Term

### The term's origins and definitional instability

The phrase "cancel culture" migrated from niche Black vernacular — where "cancelling" someone meant a humorous withdrawal of emotional investment — into mainstream political discourse around 2018. [Meredith D. Clark's etymology essay in *Data & Society*](https://datasociety.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/2020_OriginalArticle_Clark_CancelCulture.pdf) traces the term's roots in Black Twitter's practice of "dragging" public figures, arguing that the practice of *calling out* was initially a tool of agency by marginalised groups — "an expression of agency, a choice to withdraw one's attention from someone or something whose values, (in)action, or speech are so offensive" — before being counter-framed by dominant culture as a "moral panic." Clark contends the label "cancel culture" effectively redirected public discourse away from the underlying harms being contested, turning the victims of accountability into its apparent perpetrators.

Academic analysis increasingly treats the term as an **essentially contested concept** in the tradition of philosopher W. B. Gallie. [Claudio Novelli, writing in *Athena* (University of Bologna, 2023)](https://athena.unibo.it/article/download/17536/16493/68793) applies Gallie's seven criteria — appraisiveness, internal complexity, diverse describability, openness, reciprocal recognition, historical exemplars, and progressive competition — and concludes that "cancel culture" fulfils them all. The term, he argues, is best understood as an essentially contested concept (ECC): like "democracy" or "rule of law," it has a recognisable shared core yet generates "inevitable and endless" contestation over its appropriate use and evaluation. [The same argument appears in Novelli's SSRN preprint (2023)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4494040), which proposes that "intermediate agreements and principles of reasonableness" — rather than definitional victory — offer the most productive path through the debate.

### Pew Research: what the public actually thinks

The most cited empirical data on public perceptions comes from [Pew Research Center's May 2021 report, *Americans and 'Cancel Culture': Where Some See Calls for Accountability, Others See Censorship and Punishment*](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2021/05/19/americans-and-cancel-culture-where-some-see-calls-for-accountability-others-see-censorship-punishment/), based on a nationally representative survey of **10,093 US adults** conducted in September 2020. Key findings:

- Only **44%** of Americans had heard "at least a fair amount" about the phrase; **38% had heard nothing at all** — awareness was heavily skewed towards under-30s (64% familiar) and those with higher education. This means the label itself was unfamiliar to the majority of the public at the time of the survey.
- Among those *familiar* with the term, the **most common description** (49%) was accountability-focused: "actions taken to hold others accountable." Only 14% described it as censorship and 12% as "mean-spirited attacks."
- On the broader question of calling out on social media: **58%** said it "holds people accountable," while **38%** said it "punishes people who don't deserve it."
- The **partisan split is severe**: Democrats were far more likely to see callouts as accountability (75% vs 39% of Republicans); 56% of Republicans (vs 22% of Democrats) said it punishes people undeservedly.

[A follow-up Pew analysis from August 2021](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/08/17/how-americans-feel-about-cancel-culture-and-offensive-speech-in-6-charts/) added that familiarity *did not* differ significantly by party (46% of Democrats vs 44% of Republicans had heard "at least a fair amount"), but ideology within parties mattered: liberal Democrats (59%) and conservative Republicans (49%) were the most familiar. Notably, conservative Republicans were the sub-group most likely to define the term as *censorship* (26%, compared to ~10% of Democrats).

By 2022, a [second Pew survey reported by O'Dwyer's PR (June 2022)](https://www.odwyerpr.com/story/public/18047/2022-06-09/americans-remain-divided-cancel-culture.html) found awareness had risen to **61%**, but the accountability/punishment split had tightened and become more contentious: 51% now saw it as accountability, 45% as unjust punishment — the gap had closed dramatically. Crucially, the share of Democrats who saw it as *unjust punishment* had risen by 10 percentage points since 2020, suggesting the term's polarising power may be growing even within previously sympathetic audiences.

**Implication for Brehon Consensus messaging:** The Pew data reveals that the *label* "cancel culture" is not universally understood, is most familiar to the politically engaged, and predictably sorts people into opposing camps along partisan lines. Using the term risks triggering a culture-war frame before the actual substance of Brehon's approach can be communicated.

### Partisan entrenchment overrides value-based framing

[A peer-reviewed political psychology study by Fahey et al. (2024) in *Political Psychology*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/10659129241261374), drawing on Pew 2020 open-ended data and two original survey experiments on the 2021 Cooperative Election Study and a 2023 sample, found: "partisanship is a potent driver of support for and understanding of cancel culture and that value-based framing has a *weaker* impact as compared to partisanship." Even when researchers experimentally framed callout behaviour in terms of shared democratic values (free speech, accountability), partisan identification determined responses far more strongly than the framing of the argument. The researchers concluded that "cancel culture provides an important case study of how Americans process conflicting norms and values, including free speech and political accountability."

A [2025 analysis of the 2024 US presidential election (Erasmus University thesis)](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/76628/34637.pdf) found that Democratic-affiliated political actors largely **avoided the phrase altogether**, focusing instead on "accountability" and "civic responsibility" as framing terms. Republican actors, by contrast, *embraced* the term "cancel culture" as a rhetorical weapon, reframing it as a threat to free speech originating with Democrats. This divergence illustrates how the term itself has become a flexible partisan signifier rather than a neutral descriptor.

The [Public Leadership Institute](https://publicleadershipinstitute.org/2021/04/21/dont-say-cancel-culture-or-culture-wars/) explicitly advises political communicators: "Do not use the phrase 'cancel culture'... Reject the framing and insist on talking about individual instances." This is a practitioner-level acknowledgement of the problem — the label triggers associations that swamp whatever argument follows.

**Synthesis: Should Brehon use the term?** The evidence points strongly towards **evoking the *experience* without using the label**. The term "cancel culture" carries heavy partisan loading; research shows value-based reframing is less powerful than simply avoiding the charged phrase. The safer and more accurate rhetorical path is to describe the *problem* that cancel culture represents — abrupt, permanent, unilateral exclusion; no due process; no route back — without invoking its politically colonised name. This is consistent with Loretta Ross's approach (below).

### The 'call-in vs call-out' alternative: Loretta Ross

[Loretta J. Ross](https://lorettajross.com) — activist, MacArthur Fellow (2022), professor at Smith College, and author of *Calling In: How to Start Making Change with Those You'd Rather Cancel* (Simon & Schuster) — has developed the most influential practitioner framework for moving beyond cancel culture without abandoning accountability.

Ross defines her terms precisely. In her [TED Talk (2021)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xw_720iQDss):

> "You think somebody has done something wrong, you think they should be held accountable for it, and you think they should be punished for it... With this approach, you've guaranteed one thing: with this blaming and shaming, you just invited them to a fight, not a conversation, because you're publicly humiliating them."

She distinguishes between a hierarchy of responses — what she calls the **Five C's Continuum** — calling in, calling on, calling out, cancelling, and calling it off. As she explained to [Atmos magazine (2022)](https://atmos.earth/art-and-culture/calling-in-macarthur-fellow-loretta-j-ross-cancel-culture/):

> "Both calling out and calling in are accountability mechanisms — it's just how you pursue it that changes. Calling out seeks to hold someone publicly accountable for something you think they've done wrong by using the tactics of blaming, shaming, and humiliation. Cancelling is the ultimate callout... Calling in is also a way of seeking accountability, but instead of punishment, blame, and shame, you use love, respect, and conversation as a way to achieve that accountability."

Critically, Ross does *not* reject all forms of calling out. On [WNYC's *The Takeaway*](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/takeaway/segments/prof-loretta-ross-on-calling-in-calling-out), she stated: "Calling out is not a partisan behavior. Everybody does it... Calling in is a call-out done with love." Her critique is of callout as a *first* resort, not a last resort:

> "Young people fail to understand is that calling out is our tactic of last resort — not our tactic of first resort. They're going about doing the right thing the wrong way. You don't start out by putting people on blast. You start out by trying to establish some common ground."

One of her students captured the structural problem with callout culture memorably: *"A call-out is not an invitation for growth. It's the expectation that you've already grown."*

Ross's framework is directly relevant to Brehon: peer juries that begin with calling-in before escalating to callout (and cancellation only as a final, proportionate step) operationalise her Five C's Continuum as a *procedural* system. This framing positions Brehon not as anti-accountability but as *better accountability* — a distinction that should resonate across political divides.

## C2.2 Communicating Restorative Justice to General Audiences

### Howard Zehr and the founding framework

Howard Zehr is universally credited as the founding theorist of the restorative justice movement. His 1990 book *Changing Lenses: A New Focus for Crime and Justice* (Herald Press, 1990; 25th anniversary edition 2015) introduced the central paradigm shift. As [Eastern Mennonite University's profile of Zehr](https://emu.edu/now/crossroads/2015/07/20/howard-zehr-pioneer-of-restorative-justice/) notes, the book grew from his 1970s work founding the first US victim-offender reconciliation programme in Elkhart County, Indiana.

The "changing lenses" metaphor is itself a communication tool. Zehr contrasts:

- **Criminal justice lens (telephoto)**: "narrow angle of view," focused sharply on *guilt* and *punishment*, misses what lies outside its focal area — specifically, victims' needs and offenders' obligations to repair harm.
- **Restorative justice lens (wide-angle)**: "more encompassing angle of view," centres *harm*, *needs*, and *obligations to repair*, rather than abstract rule-violation and deserved suffering.

Zehr identified three pillars of restorative justice in his 2002 *Little Book of Restorative Justice* ([PDF via Zehr Institute](https://zehr-institute.org/docs/Howard-Zehr.pdf)):

1. **Harms and related needs** — of victims first, but also communities and offenders
2. **Obligations** that result from and give rise to the harm — primarily the offender's obligation to repair, but also the community's
3. **Engagement** of all those with a legitimate stake — victims, offenders, community members

His reframing of **accountability** is the crucial communication move for Brehon:

> "The criminal justice system is concerned about holding offenders accountable, but that means making sure offenders get the punishment they deserve. Little in the process encourages offenders to understand the consequences of their actions or to empathize with victims. On the contrary, the adversarial game requires offenders to look out for themselves... Restorative justice has brought an awareness of the limits and negative byproducts of punishment. Beyond that, however, it has argued that *punishment is not real accountability*. Real accountability involves facing up to what one has done. It means encouraging offenders to understand the impact of their behavior — the harms they have done — and to take steps to put things right as much as possible." ([Goodreads / *Changing Lenses* quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/author/quotes/74938.Howard_Zehr))

Zehr quotes also appear in the 2003 booklet [*The Little Book of Restorative Justice* (PDF)](https://www.antoniocasella.eu/restorative/Zehr_Gohar_2003.pdf):

> "If crime is essentially about harm, accountability means offenders must be encouraged to understand that harm, that is, to begin to comprehend the consequences of one's behavior. Moreover, it means they have a responsibility to make things right in so far as possible, both concretely and symbolically."

### The evidence base: Sherman and Strang (2007)

The gold-standard empirical review is [Lawrence W. Sherman and Heather Strang, *Restorative Justice: The Evidence* (The Smith Institute, London, 2007)](https://ajc.alaska.gov/acjc/docs/resources/restorative/restorjus.pdf), produced for the Jerry Lee Program of Randomised Trials in Restorative Justice. This report reviewed 36 direct experimental and quasi-experimental comparisons between restorative justice (RJ) and conventional criminal justice (CJ) across multiple countries. Key findings across at least two tests each:

- **Substantially reduced repeat offending** for some offenders (but not all)
- **Doubled or more** the number of offences brought to justice when used as diversion from CJ (because offenders could accept responsibility without formal conviction)
- **Reduced crime victims' post-traumatic stress symptoms** and related costs — notably, two London randomised controlled trials (RCTs) showed RJ reduces PTSD
- **More satisfaction** from both victims and offenders with the justice process compared to CJ — victims were less angry, less fearful, and more likely to receive a sincere apology
- **Reduced victims' desire for violent revenge** against offenders — consistently across four RCTs
- **Reduced recidivism more than prison** (for adults) or as well as prison (for youths)
- **Cost savings** when used as diversion from CJ

On violent crimes specifically, Sherman and Strang found six rigorous field tests demonstrating RJ reduced recidivism after adult or youth violence; three of these were RCTs. The 2007 report states bluntly: "In general, RJ seems to reduce crime more effectively with more, rather than less, serious crimes" — a finding that runs "counter to conventional wisdom."

Sherman and Strang's data was subsequently [meta-analysed by Strang et al. in the *Campbell Systematic Review*](https://restorativejustice.org.uk/sites/default/files/resources/files/Campbell%20RJ%20review.pdf), which synthesised ten eligible randomised trials of face-to-face restorative justice conferences (RJCs). Results: "RJCs cause a modest but highly cost-effective reduction in the frequency of repeat offending" (Cohen's d = -.155; p = .001); 9 out of 10 results were in the predicted direction. The UK cost-effectiveness estimate was striking: 8 times (and up to 14 times in London) more benefit in prevented crime costs than the cost of delivering RJCs.

The [Restorative Justice Council (UK) summary](https://restorativejustice.org.uk/resources/restorative-justice-evidence-%E2%80%93-professor-lawrence-sherman-and-dr-heather-strang) confirms the 2007 report as the definitive evidence review for UK practitioners.

### The 'soft on crime' misperception: a communication challenge

The most persistent barrier to public acceptance of restorative justice is the misperception that it is "letting people off." The [US Sentencing Project (2026)](https://www.sentencingproject.org/reports/restorative-justice-diversion-a-better-way-to-provide-meaningful-accountability-for-youth/) frames this directly:

> "Arguably the biggest barrier to wholesale expansion of restorative justice diversion... is a media and political environment that often fuels perceptions that restorative justice and diversion in general are soft responses to delinquent conduct... Restorative justice 'is often portrayed negatively in the media, seen as a soft option and a means for those in power to avoid punitive measures'."

The [Centre for Evidenced Legal Solutions in Restorative Justice](https://celsir.org/is-restorative-justice-soft-on-crime-debunking-a-common-myth/) responds to this directly:

> "In many conventional criminal justice systems, an offender may plead guilty through legal representation, serve a sentence, and never confront the personal impact of their actions. Restorative justice, by contrast, requires the offender to hear firsthand how the crime affected the victim, accept responsibility for the harm caused, and participate in creating a plan to repair the damage. This process can be emotionally challenging... Avoiding responsibility is not an option."

Danielle Sered, quoted by the [Sentencing Project](https://www.sentencingproject.org/reports/restorative-justice-diversion-a-better-way-to-provide-meaningful-accountability-for-youth/), articulates the counterclaim memorably:

> "Being punished only requires that people sustain the suffering imposed upon them for their transgression. It is passive... It requires neither agency nor dignity, nor does it require work."

The [US Office of Juvenile Justice (OJJDP Balanced and Restorative Justice model)](https://ojjdp.ojp.gov/sites/g/files/xyckuh176/files/pubs/implementing/accountability.html) offers the most memorable formulation for Brehon's purposes:

> **"Support without accountability leads to moral weakness. Accountability without support is a form of cruelty."** — Stan Basler, Oklahoma Conference of Churches

The [UN Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC Module 8)](https://www.unodc.org/e4j/zh/crime-prevention-criminal-justice/module-8/key-issues/4--issues-in-implementing-restorative-justice.html) acknowledges the misperception and provides the counter-evidence: "with respect to the perception that restorative justice might be an 'easy way' for offenders, evidence suggests that offenders find it more challenging to meet the victim face to face and realize the impact of their wrongdoing than going to court."

The [Innovating Justice network](https://www.innovatingjustice.org/updates/restorative-justice-rj-myths-facts/) frames the key distinction as **passive vs active accountability**: "Typical responses to crime often rely on a passive form of accountability, where a sentence or punishment is imposed on someone from the outside. Restorative justice, on the other hand, offers a more active form of accountability — where those responsible have to confront the impact of their actions, make amends, and work with those they've harmed to make sure it won't happen again."

**Communication guidance for Brehon:** The research consistently supports a specific rhetorical move: redefine "accountability" to mean *active repair*, not *passive suffering*. This directly addresses the "soft" accusation while repositioning restorative approaches as *more* demanding, not less.

## C2.3 Procedural Justice and Procedural Legitimacy

### Tom Tyler: the foundational research programme

Tom Tyler (Macklin Fleming Professor of Law and Professor of Psychology, Yale) is the dominant figure in procedural justice research. His 1990 book *Why People Obey the Law* (Yale University Press; reissued Princeton University Press 2006) launched the modern empirical literature. Based on a panel survey of Chicago residents, Tyler demonstrated that:

> People's compliance with the law is driven **not** primarily by fear of punishment or rational cost-benefit calculation, but by **ethical evaluations** — specifically, whether they perceive the legal system as legitimate. ([Books description via Google Books](https://books.google.com.qa/books?id=77G9sCO_MKIC))

As Tyler summarised in a [Yale News interview (2024)](https://news.yale.edu/2024/06/04/good-authority-yales-tyler-honored-study-legitimacy-policing):

> "What my work says is, there's a completely different way that people relate to law, which is they feel an obligation and responsibility to follow the law. It's a value within them: if the law is reasonable and appropriate, it's my responsibility to follow it. Legitimacy is the idea that people look to see if the authorities are entitled to be obeyed, and if they think they're entitled to be obeyed, then you don't have to make them do it. They do it because they think it's the right thing to do."

In a 2001 USInfo analysis of his research ([procedural justice and sense of fairness](https://usinfo.org/usia/usinfo.state.gov/journals/itdhr/0701/ijde/tyler.htm)), Tyler reported the hierarchy of compliance drivers from the 1990 Chicago study: "The strongest influence was that of morality, the second strongest influence that of legitimacy. Risk estimates also influenced compliance, but were the weakest influence of the three outlined." Put numerically: a Yale analysis referenced in [Tyler's Mexico lecture notes](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/documents/pdf/sela/Tyler_Mexico_talk_final.pdf) found that "risk explained 0–1% of the variance in rule following, while legitimacy explained 16–19% depending upon how it was conceptualized."

Tyler's subsequent work, including the chapter "Procedural Justice, Legitimacy, and the Effective Rule of Law" in *Crime and Justice* ([University of Chicago Press, 2003](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdfplus/10.1086/652233)), generalised the finding: "the key factor shaping public behavior is the fairness of the processes legal authorities use when dealing with members of the public. This reaction occurs both during personal experiences with legal authorities and when community residents are making general evaluations of the law and of legal authorities."

A [Yale Law School working paper on procedural justice and effective law enforcement](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/area/center/justice/document/5697d9ee08aea2d74375cb87.pdf) makes the implications for governance systems explicit:

> "Perceiving policing as legitimate includes having opportunities for voice and participation in designing policing policies, experiencing decision making as neutral and unbiased, receiving respectful treatment during the implementation of those policies in the community, and trusting in the benevolence and sincerity of police in their dealings with the public."

### The four pillars of procedural justice

Tyler identified four factors that consistently appear in people's assessments of whether procedures are fair. These are sometimes rendered as two paired dimensions — *fairness of decision-making* (voice, neutrality) and *fairness of interpersonal treatment* (respect, trustworthiness) — by [Blader and Tyler (2003) in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*](http://www.rpforschools.net/articles/Theory/Blader%20&%20Tyler%202003%20A%20Four-Component%20Model%20of%20Procedural%20Justice%20-%20Defining%20the%20Meaning%20of%20a%20%E2%80%9CFair%E2%80%9D%20Process.pdf). Tyler described all four in the 2024 [Yale News interview](https://news.yale.edu/2024/06/04/good-authority-yales-tyler-honored-study-legitimacy-policing):

1. **Voice** — "Do they let you explain your situation, tell your side of the story?"
2. **Neutrality** — "Do you feel they're not biased or prejudiced? They're not letting their personal feelings get involved. They're consistently applying the law in a neutral, unbiased way."
3. **Respect** — "People feel entitled to be treated with respect... Not demeaning, not insulting, not diminishing you."
4. **Trustworthiness** — "Do you think that this authority is trying to do the right thing, that they're sincerely trying to help the people involved, sincerely trying to do what's good for the community? They're not acting out of prejudice or bias."

A [Population Health Law Research (PHLR) systematic overview of procedural justice](https://phlr.org/sites/default/files/downloads/resource/CPHLR-TheoryMethods2023_ProceduralJustice.pdf) confirms: "Research has made a compelling case for the positive effect of perceptions of procedural justice on an individual's sense of a rule's legitimacy and his or her compliance with it." It further notes that "procedural justice judgments are more central to judgments of legitimacy than are such factors as the perceived effectiveness of the police in combating crime."

The [2015 President's Task Force on 21st Century Policing](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5b7ea2794cde7a79e7c00582/t/673cd0cbcc516e564f4aad37/1755622824629/social+sciences.pdf) labelled legitimacy "the first pillar of policing" and advocated procedural justice as its primary tool — a federal-level endorsement of Tyler's research programme.

### Thibaut and Walker: the original procedural justice findings

The academic origin of procedural justice research is [John Thibaut and Laurens Walker, *Procedural Justice: A Psychological Analysis* (Erlbaum, 1975)](https://academic.oup.com/book/12327/chapter/161881832). Thibaut and Walker ran experimental simulations of legal dispute resolution, comparing adversarial (US-style) and inquisitorial (European-style) procedures. Their central and "then-counterintuitive proposition" (as [Blader & Tyler 2003 describe it](http://www.rpforschools.net/articles/Theory/Blader%20&%20Tyler%202003%20A%20Four-Component%20Model%20of%20Procedural%20Justice%20-%20Defining%20the%20Meaning%20of%20a%20%E2%80%9CFair%E2%80%9D%20Process.pdf)) was: "disputants care *as much* about how their disputes are resolved as they do about the outcomes they receive."

They distinguished two types of control:

- **Decision control**: control over the final verdict (vested in the judge or third party)
- **Process control**: control over the presentation of evidence (retained by disputants)

Their key finding, as [described in the University of Waterloo's historical review of procedural justice](https://uwaterloo.ca/fairness-at-work-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/procedural_justice_an_historical_review_and_critical_analysis.pdf): "disputants preferred procedures that allowed them to present evidence for their case — thereby retaining control over the process — **even when they did not retain control over the final verdict**." Adversarial procedures were "consistently found to be preferred" over inquisitorial ones, even when experimental subjects were placed behind a Rawlsian veil of ignorance.

Crucially: "Over the next decade, researchers repeatedly replicated the finding that allowing people to provide input into decisions that affect them enhances their perceptions of procedural fairness and **increases outcome acceptance, even when the outcomes are undesirable.**" ([University of Waterloo review](https://uwaterloo.ca/fairness-at-work-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/procedural_justice_an_historical_review_and_critical_analysis.pdf))

The [Oxford Academic overview of procedural justice](https://academic.oup.com/book/12327/chapter/161881832) explicitly names the mechanism: "Thibaut and Walker's (1975) classic study demonstrated that outcome effects are neither the sole nor the strongest determinants of satisfaction with a political process."

### The 'fair process effect'

The term **"fair process effect"** was coined by Folger et al. (1979), as noted in the [University of Waterloo review](https://uwaterloo.ca/fairness-at-work-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/procedural_justice_an_historical_review_and_critical_analysis.pdf): "Folger (1977) labeled process control as 'voice' in his formative research on reward allocation, and later he and colleagues coined the term the 'fair process effect' to refer to 'cases in which greater satisfaction results from giving people a voice in decisions'."

The [Oxford Academic entry](https://academic.oup.com/book/12327/chapter/161881832) characterises the effect and its importance for governance: "A major reason why procedural justice is of interest to political theorists is its ability to lessen the impact of unpleasant decisions. This 'cushioning' effect is referred to in the literature as the 'fair-process effect' (Folger *et al.* 1979)... Researchers have noted that procedural justice effects are frequently most powerful when outcomes are negative, thereby acting to cushion the effects of negative circumstances."

The [Yale legitimacy paper](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/area/center/justice/document/ssrnpopularlegitimacy.pdf) quantifies the effect starkly: "The primary factor shaping decision acceptance (when legal authorities make decisions concerning the individual in question) is the procedural justice of the process through which a decision was reached. This factor is approximately **seven times as important** as either the favorability or the fairness of the outcome."

**Implication for Brehon:** The procedural justice literature provides the theoretical backbone for the entire Brehon design. A system that gives community members *voice* in establishing rules, that applies those rules *neutrally* and *consistently*, that treats accused parties with *respect*, and that is transparently motivated by community welfare (*trustworthiness*) — such a system will be perceived as more legitimate, and its outcomes — including unfavourable ones — will be accepted at far higher rates, regardless of whether members "win." The fair process effect means a verdict is experienced differently depending on whether the process was perceived as fair.

## C2.4 Consent, Self-Governance, and the Commons: Elinor Ostrom

### Context and significance

[Elinor Ostrom](https://www.agrariantrust.org/ostroms-eight-design-principles-for-a-successfully-managed-commons/) (1933–2012) was awarded the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences — the first woman to receive it — for her empirical work on collective governance of common-pool resources (CPRs). Her 1990 book *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* (Cambridge University Press) remains the foundational text in commons governance. Over her career she documented more than 800 cases from around the world, drawn from irrigation systems in the Philippines, village commons in Switzerland and Japan, fisheries in Maine and Turkey, and high mountain meadows across multiple continents.

Ostrom's central argument was a challenge to both the "tragedy of the commons" thesis (Hardin 1968) and the conventional prescriptions of either privatisation or top-down state control. As [the University of New Mexico's review of *Governing the Commons*](https://digitalrepository.unm.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1848&context=nrj) summarises: "Noting the numerous occasions in which common pool resources are managed successfully with neither centralised governmental control nor privatisation, Ostrom argues for a *third approach*: the design of durable cooperative institutions that are organised and governed by the resource users."

Her method was inductive — she studied which **design principles** were consistently present in commons institutions that had endured for decades or even centuries, and which were absent in systems that collapsed. The result was her famous list of eight design principles.

### The Eight Design Principles (Ostrom 1990, p. 90, Table 3.1)

The following is based on Ostrom's own formulation in *Governing the Commons*, as reproduced in the [Lincoln Institute of Land Policy chapter](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/2076_1399_LP2008-ch02-Design-Principles-of-Robust-Property-Rights-Institutions_0.pdf) and confirmed across multiple academic sources:

| # | Principle | Core logic |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Clearly defined boundaries** | The resource system and the individuals/households with rights to use it are clearly defined |
| 2 | **Proportional equivalence between benefits and costs** | Rules allocating benefits are proportional to required inputs; perceived fairness sustains cooperation |
| 3 | **Collective-choice arrangements** | Most individuals affected by the rules are included in the group that can modify them |
| 4 | **Monitoring** | Monitors actively audit conditions and user behaviour; they are accountable to users or are users themselves |
| 5 | **Graduated sanctions** | Rule violators receive graduated sanctions depending on seriousness and context of the offence |
| 6 | **Conflict resolution mechanisms** | Users and officials have rapid access to low-cost local arenas to resolve conflicts |
| 7 | **Minimal recognition of rights to organise** | External authorities do not challenge users' rights to devise their own institutions |
| 8 | **Nested enterprises** | For larger systems, governance activities are organised in multiple layers of nested enterprises |

The [Social-Ecological Systems Meta-Analysis Database (Dartmouth)](https://sesmad.dartmouth.edu/theories/54) notes that in 2010 Cox et al. conducted a meta-analysis expanding Ostrom's eight principles to ten, confirming their broad applicability.

### Principle 3: Collective-Choice Arrangements (the consent anchor)

For Brehon, Principle 3 is the most directly applicable. Ostrom found that in long-enduring commons, "most individuals affected by harvesting and protection rules are included in the group that can modify these rules." As [the PMC paper applying Ostrom to co-design (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7879991/) explains: "Group members must be able to create at least some of their own rules and make their own decisions by consensus. **People hate being told what to do but will work for group goals that they have agreed upon.**"

The [Heinrich Böll Foundation's accessible guide to Ostrom's principles](https://tn.boell.org/en/2023/04/19/5-elinor-ostrom-et-les-huit-principes-de-gestion-des-communs) makes the consent logic explicit: "Rules are more likely to be followed by people if they had a hand in writing them." This connects directly to the procedural justice evidence: participatory rule-making *is* a form of process control (voice), which drives perceived legitimacy and outcome acceptance.

Ostrom's own formulation, per the [Lincoln Institute chapter](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/2076_1399_LP2008-ch02-Design-Principles-of-Robust-Property-Rights-Institutions_0.pdf), captures both the design and the reason:

> "Most individuals affected by harvesting and protection rules are included in the group that can modify these rules."

The experimental literature confirms this mechanism. [Van Klingeren and Buskens (2023, Utrecht University)](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/438057/van-klingeren-buskens-2023-graduated-sanctioning-endogenous-institutions-and-sustainable-cooperation-in-common-pool.pdf) report: "Dal Bó et al. (2010) show in a series of prisoner's dilemma experiments that endogenously chosen rules have a greater effect on player behaviour than exogenously imposed rules." Further: "there is experimental evidence that punishments and rewards allocated to others via endogenous sanctioning institutions are perceived as more fair and acceptable, and that as a consequence these chosen institutions perform better in increasing adherence to the rules in social dilemmas."

Principle 7 reinforces this: the right to self-organise must be recognised by external authorities. Ostrom found that externally imposed rules are "unlikely to be adapted to local circumstances and violate principle 3" — they undermine both the practical fit of rules and the sense of ownership that makes them self-enforcing. ([PMC 2021 summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7879991/))

### Principle 5: Graduated Sanctions (the restorative anchor)

This is the principle most directly relevant to Brehon's design philosophy. Ostrom's fifth design principle states: "Users who violate rules in use are likely to receive graduated sanctions (depending on the seriousness and context of the offense) from other users, from officials accountable to these users, or from both." ([Lincoln Institute, quoting Ostrom 1990 Table 3.1](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/2076_1399_LP2008-ch02-Design-Principles-of-Robust-Property-Rights-Institutions_0.pdf))

Ostrom's extended discussion of graduated sanctions in *Governing the Commons* (pp. 94–100) is notable for how explicitly she reasons about human psychology. [Van Klingeren and Buskens (2023)](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/438057/van-klingeren-buskens-2023-graduated-sanctioning-endogenous-institutions-and-sustainable-cooperation-in-common-pool.pdf) summarise Ostrom's argument:

> "She argues that for some violators, a small penalty may be enough to remind them of the importance of cooperation and compliance with the rules, **while a large sanction may lead to feelings of unfairness and, consequently, to more rule-breaking behaviour** (Ostrom, 1990)."

And crucially, Ostrom's observation about the *initial* sanction:

> "She states that in many enduring self-governing CPR systems the first sanction after an offence is so low it barely has an impact on the offender. This initial sanction can be thought of as a *reminder* for the offender — and other resource users — that breaking the rules will get you caught, but that there is still trust that the offender will cooperate from then on. Ostrom argues that a large fine after a first offence may lead to **resentment instead of cooperation**."

[Makecommoningwork.fed.wiki](http://www.makecommoningwork.fed.wiki/graduated-sanctions-according-to-ostrom.html) — a practitioner wiki dedicated to Ostrom's work — cites from *Governing the Commons* (p. 97): "The appropriator-monitor may want to impose only a modest sanction... A small penalty may be sufficient to remind the infractor of the importance of compliance. The monitor might be in a similar situation in the future and would want some understanding at that time."

The [Dartmouth SESMAD database](https://sesmad.dartmouth.edu/theories/54) defines graduated sanctions: "Graduated self-sanctions are needed to deter rule-breaking without **diminishing social capital with excessive punishment**."

One further important qualification comes from [Delft University research on sanctions in the commons](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_c94ebfb1-e9be-4a0a-8478-3a077e606934): the key insight may be less about the *graduation* per se than about the *initial forgiveness*: "the significance of graduated sanctions is not so much that they are sanctions but that they are initially forgiving." This is directly congruent with Zehr's restorative justice framework and Ross's calling-in model.

### Principle 6: Conflict Resolution Mechanisms

Ostrom's sixth principle requires that users and officials have "rapid access to low-cost local arenas to resolve conflicts among users or between users and officials." ([Lincoln Institute](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/2076_1399_LP2008-ch02-Design-Principles-of-Robust-Property-Rights-Institutions_0.pdf)) The [Böll Foundation guide](https://tn.boell.org/en/2023/04/19/5-elinor-ostrom-et-les-huit-principes-de-gestion-des-communs) adds: "When issues arise within a community, solutions for mediation and conflict resolution should be informal and affordable for everyone." The [SESMAD entry](https://sesmad.dartmouth.edu/theories/54) specifies: "Conflict resolution mechanisms are needed to resolve differences over rule-breaking so that a community can move forward in a way that is *considered fair and acceptable by those involved*."

The "fair and acceptable" qualifier connects Ostrom's empirical findings on long-enduring commons directly to Tyler's procedural justice research: the mechanisms that make conflict resolution perceived as fair (voice, neutrality, respect, trust) are structurally identical to the principles of procedural justice.

### Why Ostrom is the key anchor for Brehon

Ostrom's work is uniquely powerful for Brehon because it provides:

1. **Empirical grounding across diverse cultures and scales** — not a theoretical model but observed features of successful real-world governance institutions
2. **Nobel-level authority** — hard to dismiss as idealistic or untested
3. **Direct support for graduated/restorative sanctions** — not as a soft option but as empirically observed in the most *robust and long-lasting* institutions
4. **Direct support for collective-choice / consent** — community rule-making is not just philosophically desirable but instrumentally necessary for compliance
5. **The "externally imposed rules fail" finding** — a direct rebuke to top-down moderation systems, validating Brehon's community-consent architecture
6. **The "large sanction leads to resentment and more rule-breaking" finding** — empirical validation for proportionality and graduated response

The full structure of Brehon Consensus can be mapped directly onto Ostrom's principles: clearly defined community membership (Principle 1); community-written rules adapted to local norms (Principles 2 and 3); peer monitoring (Principle 4); graduated sanctions with a route back (Principle 5); the jury process as low-cost conflict resolution (Principle 6); community autonomy from platform-level override (Principle 7); and potential federation with other Brehon communities (Principle 8).

For non-technical users, the move from "social network" to "the fediverse" feels like a bait-and-switch into jargon. The phrase "decentralised" appears on the page but is not defined; users arriving from mainstream platforms may not understand it.

The phrase "jointly owned and democratically-controlled enterprise" — while legally precise — uses "enterprise" as a category noun, which carries commercial connotations that sit oddly with the social mission framing in the same sentence. The lack of any visitor-facing messaging on the main homepage is a significant gap for public communication.

## D.10 Extracted Patterns: What Works When Explaining Democratic/Governance/Federated Tools to Non-Technical People

Drawing across all eleven projects and their messaging, the following recurring techniques are identifiably effective.

---

### Pattern 1 — Lead with the Power Contrast, Not the Feature

The most effective single-sentence pitches in this set all name a power relationship rather than a product feature:

- Mastodon: "Social networking **that's not for sale**" (power: commodity vs. people-owned)
- Nostr: "controlled by **users**, not **platforms**" (power: individual vs. institution)
- platform.coop: "**stakeholders** before **shareholders**" (power: community vs. capital)
- Cobudget: "**Shared Power** over Money" (power: distributed vs. concentrated)

Each of these works by naming *who is in charge now* (corporations, platforms, shareholders) and *who should be in charge instead* (people, users, stakeholders). The contrast requires no technical vocabulary. The reader does not need to understand federation, open source, or DAOs — only that there is a power transfer happening.

**Lesson for Brehon Consensus:** Define the power contrast explicitly. Not "a governance layer" but "governance by the community, not the platform."

---

### Pattern 2 — The Telephone Analogy (and What Makes Analogies Work)

The fedi.tips telephone-network explanation is the cleanest piece of public communication in this dataset for a complex technical idea. It works because:

1. **The source domain is universal and pre-existing in lived experience** — everyone has made a phone call across carriers.
2. **The analogy maps onto the technical reality precisely** — the carrier-to-carrier connection is structurally identical to the server-to-server federation.
3. **It ends with the user's experience, not the engineer's** — "they have no idea they are talking to someone on another provider" resolves the complexity by saying it *does not matter to you* in practice.

Similarly, Nostr's HTTP/TCP-IP analogy works for developers because they have internalised it. The mistake is using a developer analogy for a lay audience.

**Lesson for Brehon Consensus:** Find an analogy where the bridge between familiar experience and new concept is *structurally precise*, not merely poetic. Good governance analogies: village councils, co-op AGMs, jury deliberation, credit unions (all combine participation, accountability, and known outcomes without technical vocabulary).

---

### Pattern 3 — Concrete Verbs Over Abstract Nouns

Compare these phrases:

| Weak (abstract noun) | Strong (concrete verb) |
|---|---|
| "citizen participation" (Consul, Decidim) | "discusses proposals, votes with reasoning" (Loomio) |
| "collaborative money management" (Open Collective) | "raise, manage and disburse" (Open Collective tagline) |
| "governance procedures" (PolicyKit) | "automatically carry out those procedures" (PolicyKit) |
| "field of study" (IIRP) | "bring those harmed … into communication" (RJC) |

The RJC definition stands out as an exemplar: every clause in "restorative justice brings those harmed by crime or conflict and those responsible for the harm into communication, enabling everyone affected … to play a part in repairing the harm and finding a positive way forward" is human-action-oriented. There are no system nouns — only people doing things. Loomio's micro-lines ("Every voice heard", "Decisions that stick") are similarly verb-derived — "heard" and "stick" are outcomes, not features.

**Lesson for Brehon Consensus:** Write governance tools in verbs of community action: *discuss, decide, repair, appeal, propose, vote, record*. Not "consensus mechanism" but "the community decides".

---

### Pattern 4 — Name the Pain Before Naming the Solution

Loomio's gap analogy is the clearest example of this pattern: "Lost in email. Too fast in chat. Endless in meetings." The pain is named in three punchy phrases before Loomio is mentioned at all. The solution then appears as an obvious answer rather than a marketed claim.

Why Me? does the same at a deeper register: the hero question "Are you a victim of crime?" does not explain restorative justice — it meets the reader where they are, in their own experience of harm.

Cobudget's about-page version: "from spreadsheets into software prototypes" names the pain of ad hoc collaborative finance before the tool is introduced.

**Lesson for Brehon Consensus:** Name the governance pain first: fractured moderation, opaque bans, unilateral decisions, no record of why rules changed. Let the reader arrive at the solution by recognising their own situation.

---

### Pattern 5 — "Owned By You" and Variants

The phrase "owned by you" (or its equivalent) appears across multiple projects as the single most effective shorthand for the difference between open/democratic/federated tools and platform capitalism:

- Mastodon: "back in the hands of the people"
- platform.coop: "shared platform ownership by workers and users"
- Open Collective: "community-owned platform" / "exit to community"
- ICA definition: "jointly owned and democratically-controlled"
- Cobudget: "open infrastructure for the common good"

Each variant uses *ownership* as the frame, not *technology* or *governance* or *decentralisation*. Ownership is a concept with legal, moral, and emotional weight that is universally understood.

**Lesson for Brehon Consensus:** Ownership language likely outperforms governance language for non-technical audiences. "Your community's rules, not ours" carries more immediate meaning than "community governance layer."

---

### Pattern 6 — The "Not For Sale / Not for Profit" Anchor

Several of the strongest taglines in this set work by explicitly naming a disavowal of the commercial model:

- Mastodon: "Social networking that's not for sale"
- Open Collective: "money should move in the open, in service of communities" (implicit: not in service of investors)
- platform.coop: "an alternative to venture capital-funded and centralized platforms"
- Metagov: "self-governance in the digital age" (implicit: not platform governance)

The commercial-disavowal move is effective because it creates an immediate contrast with the most widely understood technology companies (Facebook, Twitter/X, Uber) without requiring any explanation of what decentralisation is. The target user already has a feeling of unease about the extractive platform model; naming that disavowal creates instant recognition.

**Lesson for Brehon Consensus:** Naming what Brehon Consensus is *not* (not controlled by the platform, not extracting your community's data, not sold to the next acquirer) may be as important as naming what it *is*.

---

### Pattern 7 — Historical/Legal Legitimacy Anchors

Several projects root their claim to legitimacy in a prior tradition, implicitly countering the "experimental" or "risky" objection:

- platform.coop: "almost 200-year-old history of cooperatives" / "The Rochdale Principles of 1844"
- PolicyKit: "Inspired by Nobel economist Elinor Ostrom"
- ICA: "Created in 1895"
- RJC: "a wider field called restorative practice" (implies an established discipline)

These anchors do the work of reassurance: *this is not a startup pivot, it is the continuation of a proven tradition.* For institutional buyers (governments, foundations, professional bodies) this is especially powerful.

**Lesson for Brehon Consensus:** Brehon law itself — an ancient Irish legal tradition centred on communal arbitration and restorative principles — is a powerful legitimacy anchor. The project name already contains it; the messaging should make it explicit and explain *why this 1,500-year-old tradition is newly relevant to digital communities.*

---

### Pattern 8 — The "For Everyone" Inclusion Signal

Why Me?, the RJC, and Metagov each use explicit inclusion language:

- RJC: "enabling everyone affected … to play a part"
- Why Me?: "for everyone affected by crime and conflict"
- Metagov: "communities that enable self-governance"
- Cobudget: "the people affected by decisions deserve an active voice in shaping them"

"Everyone" and "people affected" are key phrases. They create a broad tent that does not require expertise, status, or technical skill. Contrast with PolicyKit's "Author custom community governance procedures with … short programming scripts" — which does the opposite.

**Lesson for Brehon Consensus:** The governance layer should be positioned as accessible to every community member, not just moderators, admins, or technically skilled users. Messaging should make this explicit.

## E.1 Graeber & Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything* (2021): Thesis and Critiques

### What the Book Actually Argues

*The Dawn of Everything: A New History of Humanity* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux / Allen Lane, 2021, 704 pp.) was written by the late anthropologist David Graeber and archaeologist David Wengrow. It became an international bestseller, entered the *New York Times* bestseller list at No. 2 in November 2021, and was awarded the J.I. Staley Prize by the School for Advanced Research in 2025 "in recognition of exceptional scholarship and writing that expand the boundaries of anthropological thought" ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

**The central thesis** is that the dominant story of human social development — small egalitarian hunter-gatherer bands → agriculture → hierarchy → states — is not merely false but "politically limiting," because it presents the present as inevitable ([Ideasthesia summary](https://www.ideasthesia.org/graeber-dawn-of-everything/)). The authors argue instead that the archaeological and anthropological record reveals *an almost inexhaustible capacity for self-conscious social and political experimentation* across tens of thousands of years ([Protean Magazine interview with Wengrow, 2022](https://proteanmagstaging.wpcomstaging.com/2022/03/28/primordial-freedoms-an-interview-with-david-wengrow/)). Key planks of this argument include:

1. **No single origin point for inequality.** The transition from foraging to agriculture took millennia and frequently ran in reverse. People adopted farming, abandoned it, and combined it with foraging. Agriculture did not automatically produce hierarchy ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

2. **No inevitable link between scale and domination.** The authors examine large early sites — including the Ukrainian mega-sites (Taljanky, Nebelivka) and Teotihuacan — and argue these communities functioned for extended periods without palaces, temples, centralised storage, or written administration. Teotihuacan is presented as a city that began hierarchically but "reversed course to follow more egalitarian trajectories" ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

3. **"Seasonal" or dual-mode societies.** A particularly important claim for the Brehon Consensus project is the evidence — drawn partly from Kwakiutl winter/summer alternations documented by Franz Boas — that some societies rotated seasonally between more hierarchical and more egalitarian forms of governance, demonstrating that social structure was treated as a *deliberate choice* rather than a fixed condition. The *Dobrepanstwo* summary notes Wengrow describes Ukrainian mega-sites as communities "often shifting their management models depending on the season" ([Dobrepanstwo.org, January 2026](https://dobrepanstwo.org/szkatulka-kosztownosci/wolnosc-i-dominacja-nowa-wizja-dziejow-graebera-i-wengrowa?lang=en)).

4. **Indigenous critique of European civilisation.** The authors argue that Enlightenment ideas about liberty and equality arose partly from European encounters with Indigenous North American critics such as the Wendat leader Kondiaronk, whose arguments (recorded in the Baron Lahontan's widely-read works) challenged European hierarchy, property, and punitive law. They trace how the *image* of the "noble savage" was a later European distortion of what were in fact sophisticated political arguments ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

5. **Early humans as conscious political actors.** The book's most direct relevance to Brehon Consensus is its insistence that early peoples "debated how to live, organised themselves deliberately, [and] observed neighbouring societies and often defined themselves in opposition" ([Ideasthesia summary](https://www.ideasthesia.org/graeber-dawn-of-everything/)).

### The Three Primordial Freedoms

Graeber and Wengrow identify three "substantive freedoms" that they argue characterised many pre-modern societies and whose gradual erosion explains how societies became "stuck" in hierarchical ruts:

> **1. The freedom to move away** — in a world of relatively abundant land and flexible group membership, the ability to leave coercive arrangements was itself a structural constraint on power. Leaders who became oppressive simply lost followers.
>
> **2. The freedom to disobey** — without dedicated enforcement mechanisms, collective decisions were advisory. Among groups like the Wendat, a chief could only lead through persuasion; coercive compliance was structurally impossible.
>
> **3. The freedom to create or transform social arrangements** — the capacity to reorganise society seasonally, or to experiment deliberately with different governance models, including the self-conscious *rejection* of hierarchy observed archaeologically at some sites ([Ideasthesia summary](https://www.ideasthesia.org/graeber-dawn-of-everything/); [Protean Magazine interview, 2022](https://proteanmagstaging.wpcomstaging.com/2022/03/28/primordial-freedoms-an-interview-with-david-wengrow/)).

Wengrow has explicitly stated these concepts are empirical generalisations rather than a definitive framework: "I'm sure there's a fourth freedom and a fifth freedom out there to be discovered" ([Protean Magazine interview, 2022](https://proteanmagstaging.wpcomstaging.com/2022/03/28/primordial-freedoms-an-interview-with-david-wengrow/)).

The book defines three corresponding mechanisms of domination (relevant to understanding how hierarchy becomes entrenched): **sovereignty** (control of violence), **bureaucracy** (control of information), and **charismatic politics** (competitive personal authority). Modern states, the authors argue, uniquely combine all three in ways that permanently eliminate the three freedoms above ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

---

### Scholarly Critiques — Read These Before Citing

*The Dawn of Everything* received substantial academic criticism alongside its acclaim. The following named critics and their specific objections must be understood by anyone citing the book in rigorous work:

**Gary M. Feinman** (Field Museum) accused Graeber and Wengrow of using "cherry-picked and selectively presented examples." **Michael E. Smith** (Arizona State University), an archaeologist, criticised "problems of evidence and argumentation." Both are quoted in *Anthropology Today* ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

**Ian Morris** (Stanford), a prominent archaeologist, wrote in the *American Journal of Archaeology* (Vol. 126, No. 3, 2022) — one of the field's most prestigious venues — in a review entitled "Against Method": "There is just no method here." He acknowledged the book is "well-researched" and "a work of careful research and tremendous originality" but concluded that "authors of global syntheses can easily cherry-pick details and arrange them to tell almost any story imaginable," and that Graeber and Wengrow "sometimes slide from offering tentative hypotheses into treating them as certainties." The full review is accessible at [doi:10.1086/720603](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/720603).

**Kwame Anthony Appiah** (NYU) wrote in the *New York Review of Books* that there is "a discordance between what the book says and what its sources say," though he ultimately concluded — after an extended published exchange with Wengrow — that the book's argument against historical determinism was "immensely valuable" ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

**Arjun Appadurai** (sociologist), writing in *Anthropology Today*, accused the book of "swerving to avoid a host of counter-examples and counter-arguments," though he also described its "fable" as "compelling" ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

**Walter Scheidel** (Stanford historian) criticised the book for an "excessively idealist view of historical dynamics" and "rhetorical strategies that misguide their audience," resulting in an "inability to account for broad trajectories of human development" ([Walter Scheidel review, davidgraeber.org](https://davidgraeber.org/reviews/walter-scheidel-review-of-the-dawn-of-everything/)).

**David A. Bell** (Princeton historian) accused the authors of coming "perilously close to scholarly malpractice" in their specific arguments about Indigenous origins of Enlightenment thought and Jean-Jacques Rousseau ([Wikipedia — *The Dawn of Everything*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dawn_of_Everything)).

A Marxist-oriented review in *Historical Materialism* (2023) argued the book has "evidentiary problems, misrepresenting some facts and ignoring others," dismisses key theoretical frameworks (mode of production; structure-and-agency dialectic) without offering coherent alternatives, and exhibits "a dangerous naïveté" that "fails to serve activists and theorists alike" — noting that what is *not* told "may be as consequential to our understanding as what they do" ([Historical Materialism, 'Everything Goes', 2023](https://www.historicalmaterialism.org/everything-goes-three-problems-with-the-dawn-of-everything-a-review-of-the-dawn-of-everything-by-david-graeber-and-david-wengrow/)).

**Recommended citation approach:** *The Dawn of Everything* can be cited for its general critique of the linear/deterministic narrative of state formation, and for the concept of the three primordial freedoms as an empirical generalisation. It **should not** be cited as settled evidence for any specific archaeological site or specific society without independent verification through primary sources. The book's own reviewers in leading journals acknowledge cherry-picking as a genuine concern.

## E.2 Early Irish Brehon Law: Accurate Features and Honest Problems

### Overview and Primary Sources

The most authoritative modern introduction to Brehon law is **Fergus Kelly**, *A Guide to Early Irish Law*, Early Irish Law Series vol. 3 (Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 1988; reprinted 2009). The definitive primary text compilation is **D.A. Binchy**, *Corpus Iuris Hibernici* (Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 1978), which compiles and re-edits the major law tracts. The most important specialist works on suretyship and legal procedure are **Robin Chapman Stacey** (Professor of History, University of Washington), *The Road to Judgment: From Custom to Court in Medieval Ireland and Wales* (University of Pennsylvania Press, 1994) — on personal suretyship in medieval Ireland and Wales, awarded the John Nicholas Brown Prize — and *Dark Speech: The Performance of Law in Early Ireland* (University of Pennsylvania Press, 2007), on the role of speech and performance in maintaining social order ([University of Washington Department of History](https://history.washington.edu/people/robin-chapman-stacey)). For a concise accessible summary, see also John Biggins, "Afterlives of the 'Brehon laws'", *History Ireland* (2024) ([History Ireland](https://historyireland.com/afterlives-of-the-brehon-laws/)).

Brehon law operated across Gaelic Ireland from approximately the early medieval period (texts largely compiled in the 7th–8th centuries CE) until it was formally displaced by English common law via a 1605 proclamation of King James I, though it persisted in practice in Gaelic-administered territories into the 17th century ([History Ireland, Biggins, 2024](https://historyireland.com/afterlives-of-the-brehon-laws/); [Wikipedia — Early Irish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Irish_law)).

### Features Relevant to Brehon Consensus

#### Absence of Centralised State Enforcement

This is the most legally significant feature for the project. The Brehon system operated **without a standing police force, public prosecutor, or centralised enforcement apparatus**. The Brehon (*brithem*, pl. *brithemain*) was an arbitrator, expounder of law, and professional jurist — *not* a judge in the modern public-authority sense ([Britannica — Brehon laws](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Brehon-laws)). As Biggins (2024) summarises: "In the main, the Brehon laws were not issued by a central royal authority … Elaborate procedures for personal pledging (*gell*), the use of guarantors and property distraint (*athgabál*) were designed to resolve disputes at the community level." ([History Ireland, Biggins, 2024](https://historyireland.com/afterlives-of-the-brehon-laws/)).

In the words of the Irish Criminal Defense Wiki, "under this system parties were required to agree in advance to abide by their judgments and there was no official enforcement of decisions" ([Irish Criminal Defense Wiki](http://defensewiki.ibj.org/index.php/Ireland)).

#### Suretyship: *Naidm*, *Ráth*, and *Aitire*

Contract enforcement was achieved through a sophisticated system of **three distinct forms of surety**, described in detail by Kelly (1988) and analysed at length by Stacey (1994):

- **Ráth** (paying surety / *rath*): an individual who guaranteed with their own property that a contracting party would fulfil their obligations. If the principal defaulted, the other party gave notice of intent to distrain the *ráth*'s property; the *ráth* could respond by giving a pledge while pursuing the principal ([David Friedman summary of Kelly, daviddfriedman.com](http://www.daviddfriedman.com/Academic/Course_Pages/legal_systems_very_different_12/Book_Draft/Systems/IrishLawChapter.html)). The *ráth*'s maximum liability was capped at his own honour-price.

- **Naidm** (enforcing surety): unlike the *ráth*, the *naidm* had a more active enforcement role — seizing the assets of a defaulting party. A particularly interesting provision held that a "exile of God" (*deorad Dé*), a person of exceptional spiritual status, was obliged to act as *naidm* in cases bound by the gospel or the heavenly host ([procesosdemercado.codexcafe.net — Ethics and Economics of Celtic Ireland](https://procesosdemercado.codexcafe.net/index.php/inicio/article/download/279/351/624)).

- **Aitire** (hostage surety): a person who placed themselves as physical guarantee — giving themselves as a kind of living pledge. The *aitire* was also the primary mechanism for cross-border dispute resolution under *cairde* (treaty) arrangements between *túatha* (petty kingdoms). A King making a *cairde* would swear it "on his own [namely] payment and releasing an *aitire*" ([Jacqueline Bemmer, Oxford/IHR, 2015, brehonacademy.org](https://brehonacademy.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/The-Early-Irish-Hostage-Surety-and-Inter-Territorial-Alliances-Jaqueline-Bemmer-Institute-of-Historical-Research-2015.pdf)).

This suretyship system is directly analogous to modern peer-to-peer accountability mechanisms and forms the structural backbone of a consent-based, non-state dispute-resolution system.

#### Honour-Price (*Eneclann* / *Lóg n-Enech*)

Every person in Brehon society had an *lóg n-enech* (literally "the price of their face" — commonly translated as *honour-price*). This served multiple interlocking functions:

- It determined the *maximum* sum for which a person could contract or stand surety, acting as a kind of creditworthiness ceiling ([Maynooth University PDF](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/5663/1/NH-Brehon-Law.pdf)).
- It determined the compensation owed to that person for major wrongs: murder, satire, serious injury, refusal of hospitality, theft, and violation of their protection (Kelly, 1988, summarised at [David Friedman site](http://www.daviddfriedman.com/Academic/Course_Pages/legal_systems_very_different_12/Book_Draft/Systems/IrishLawChapter.html)).
- It determined the evidential weight of a person's oath in disputes — a high-ranking person could "overswear" a lower-ranking person, meaning their sworn testimony outweighed the other's automatically.
- Honour-price could be reduced or lost entirely for specified misconduct: swearing a false oath, a king showing cowardice in battle, a bishop engaging in sexual immorality, a fraudulent poet, a dishonest lord ([procesosdemercado.codexcafe.net](https://procesosdemercado.codexcafe.net/index.php/inicio/article/download/279/351/624)).

#### Restorative Justice and Restitution (*Aithgein*)

Brehon justice was "almost exclusively based on a fine / compensation system" rather than on imprisonment or punitive retribution ([Maynooth University PDF](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/5663/1/NH-Brehon-Law.pdf)). There was no strict separation between what we would call civil and criminal law — all wrongs, including killing, generated compensation obligations. A wrongful killer owed *éraic* (body-fine/wergild, fixed at 7 *cumals* regardless of the victim's rank) plus an additional payment calculated by reference to the honour-price of the victim's kin ([David Friedman site](http://www.daviddfriedman.com/Academic/Course_Pages/legal_systems_very_different_12/Book_Draft/Systems/IrishLawChapter.html)).

A particularly notable form of restorative justice was **sick-maintenance** (*othrus*): a person who had wrongfully and seriously injured another was obliged to provide — and pay for — the victim's convalescence, a substitute worker for the victim's labour, and safe return once recovered. Three pledges were given at the outset to guarantee each obligation ([History Ireland, Biggins, 2024](https://historyireland.com/afterlives-of-the-brehon-laws/)).

#### Distraint (*Athgabál*)

If compensation was not forthcoming after judgement, the injured party could undertake **formal distraint** — seizure of the wrongdoer's property (normally cattle). The procedure was procedurally regulated: formal notice had to be given first, the notice period varied with the seriousness of the wrong, and a professional law agent was required to be present during seizure to verify its legitimacy. The defendant could at any point discharge the obligation by paying, or by giving a surety or pledge ([Maynooth University PDF](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/5663/1/NH-Brehon-Law.pdf)).

An alternative mechanism against a high-status person who might be too powerful to distrain was **fasting** (*troscadh*): the wronged party fasted outside the defendant's house from sunset to sunrise, creating significant social and reputational pressure. If the defendant ate during the fast they were obliged to pay double; failure to respond to a fast caused the loss of the right to receive compensation for any future wrong committed against the unresponsive defendant ([Maynooth University PDF](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/5663/1/NH-Brehon-Law.pdf)).

### Honest Problems: What Brehon Law Was Not

**⚠ CAUTION — the following features must be presented alongside the positive features above, or the account becomes romanticism:**

#### Rigid Social Hierarchy
Brehon society was "hierarchical and inegalitarian," divided into clearly ranked classes: kings (*rí*), nobles (*flaith*), freemen, and several grades of unfree person ([Maynooth University PDF](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/5663/1/NH-Brehon-Law.pdf)). Legal capacity — what you could contract for, the weight of your oath, the compensation you could receive — depended entirely on your rank. Irish law "did not subscribe to the Roman policy that all citizens were equal before the law" ([ipl.org Brehon Law essay](https://www.ipl.org/essay/Brrehon-Law-Analysis-FJVJRTNGZTG)). A high-ranking person's oath automatically outweighed a lower-ranking person's, structurally favouring the powerful in disputes.

#### Slavery
Slavery was a real and documented feature of early Irish society. Kelly (1988) noted that "numerous references to slavery in law-tracts, wisdom-texts, saints' Lives, annals, and sagas" attest to its "considerable importance … in early Irish society" (quoted in [Women and Slavery in Early Irish Laws, journal.fi](https://journal.fi/scf/article/download/7439/5788/17820)). Slaves (*mug* — male slave; *cumal* — female slave, which also became a unit of currency) had no honour-price except in narrow specific circumstances, could not make contracts, could not act as witnesses, and "killing your slave was not a crime in medieval Ireland" ([journal.fi article](https://journal.fi/scf/article/download/7439/5788/17820)). David Graeber, in *The Dawn of Everything*, notes that the use of *cumal* (female slave) as a currency term "indicates a significant trade in female slaves was present not long before the laws were written" ([Wikipedia — Early Irish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Irish_law)).

#### Unfree Classes
Below the free population were several grades of semi-free and unfree persons: the **fuidir** (a semi-freeman who could leave a lord but could not make independent contracts); the **bothach** (cottier); and the **senchléithe** (hereditary serf), who became legally bound to the lord's land after three or four generations of the family remaining with the same lord, and was sold with the land if the lord sold it ([Oxford University Press/Charles-Edwards chapter on the fuidir](https://academic.oup.com/book/27566/chapter/197583210); [libraryireland.com](https://www.libraryireland.com/Brehon-Laws/Non-Free.php)).

#### Women's Legal Status
Women's status under Brehon law is genuinely contested among scholars. A typical woman "did not carry an honour-price: a position shared with children, the insane, slaves, and others," though there were important exceptions for women of rank ([Wikipedia — Early Irish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Irish_law)). Women generally could not serve as witnesses, swear oaths, make contracts without male guardianship, or serve as sureties ([David Friedman site](http://www.daviddfriedman.com/Academic/Course_Pages/legal_systems_very_different_12/Book_Draft/Systems/IrishLawChapter.html)). A husband was legally permitted to physically correct his wife, though compensation was owed if a mark was left and divorce was available on multiple grounds ([Wikipedia — Early Irish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Irish_law)). Women did have *more* legal autonomy than in many contemporary European systems — property was held separately, divorce was available, and there were female judges and poets (rare) — but presenting Brehon law as proto-feminist would be historically inaccurate.

### Summary Quotation (Accurate and Usable)

A sober formulation suitable for citation in the Brehon Consensus project:

> *Early Irish Brehon law operated for roughly a millennium without a centralised enforcement apparatus. Obligations were enforced through an interlocking system of suretyship (naidm, ráth, aitire), pledges, distraint, and social sanction. Wrongs were met with restitution rather than imprisonment. The system was sophisticated, durable, and genuinely consent-based in its enforcement mechanisms. It was also, however, a product of a hierarchical, slave-holding, patriarchal society: its legal capacities and protections were calibrated precisely to social rank. Non-hierarchical it was not; non-state-enforced it genuinely was.*

## E.3 Modern Legal Cases: Customary and Indigenous Law Successfully Invoked

### E.3.1 *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* [1992] HCA 23 — Australia

**Case:** *Mabo and Others v The State of Queensland (No 2)* [1992] HCA 23; (1992) 175 CLR 1. High Court of Australia. Decided 3 June 1992.

**What happened:** Eddie Koiki Mabo and four other Torres Strait Islanders, representing the Meriam people of Mer (Murray Island), challenged the fiction that Australia was *terra nullius* — "land belonging to no one" — at the time of British settlement. The case ran for ten years, from 1982 to 1992.

**Outcome:** By a majority of 6-1 (Dawson J dissenting), the High Court held that:
- The doctrine of *terra nullius* did not apply to Australia at the time of British settlement.
- Native title — derived from the traditional customs and laws of Indigenous groups — exists as part of the common law of Australia.
- The Meriam people were "entitled as against the whole world to possession, occupation, use and enjoyment of (most of) the lands of the Murray Islands."
- Native title could, however, be extinguished by a valid exercise of government power inconsistent with ongoing native title interests.

The Keating Government passed the *Native Title Act 1993* (Cth) the following year, providing a framework for native title claims across Australia ([AIATSIS — The Mabo Case](https://aiatsis.gov.au/explore/mabo-case); [AustLII full text](https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/cases/cth/HCA/1992/23.html)).

**Relevance:** This is the clearest common-law precedent for courts recognising that a *system* of Indigenous law and custom — governing land rights, use, and obligations — continued to be legally operative despite colonisation. The customary law was not merely cited as historical curiosity; it was recognised as the *source* of ongoing legal rights.

**Caution:** The decision also confirmed that native title *can* be readily extinguished by legislative act or inconsistent Crown grant, and that where extinguished before 1975 no compensation was owed. The rights recognised are limited to the traditional laws and customs of the specific group. The decision is more accurately characterised as recognising *survival of* indigenous customary law within the colonial common law than as a wholesale revival or equivalence.

### E.3.2 *Ellis v R* [2022] NZSC 114 and [2022] NZSC 115 — New Zealand (Tikanga Māori)

**Case:** *Ellis v R* [2022] NZSC 114 (continuance reasons) and [2022] NZSC 115 (substantive appeal). New Zealand Supreme Court. Decided 7 October 2022.

**Background:** Peter Ellis had been convicted of historical child sexual offending and died before his appeal was heard. The preliminary question was whether his appeal should continue posthumously. In determining this, the Court addressed the place of tikanga Māori in New Zealand common law.

**Outcome on tikanga:** The Court held, unanimously, that tikanga has been and will continue to be recognised in the development of the common law of Aotearoa/New Zealand in cases where it is relevant. The majority (Winkelmann CJ, Glazebrook and Williams JJ) went further and held that:
- Tikanga Māori was the *first law* of Aotearoa/New Zealand and continues to shape and regulate the lives of Māori.
- The previous "colonial tests" for incorporation of tikanga into common law no longer apply. The relationship between tikanga and the common law will evolve contextually, case by case.
- Tikanga may be *determinative* in deciding future cases, not merely a value to be weighed among others.

On the substantive appeal, the Court quashed all of Ellis's convictions on the basis of a substantial miscarriage of justice (flawed expert evidence and inadequate jury direction on contamination risks in child testimony) ([Courts of New Zealand — full judgment PDF](https://www.courtsofnz.govt.nz/assets/cases/2022/2022-NZSC-114.pdf); [Chapman Tripp analysis](https://chapmantripp.com/trends-insights/weaving-tikanga-maori-and-the-common-law-into-new-cloth/); [New Zealand Law Association analysis](https://www.thelawassociation.nz/incorporating-tikanga-what-ellis-will-be-remembered-for/)).

**Relevance:** This is the most significant common-law precedent for treating an Indigenous legal tradition — tikanga, the system of Māori values, principles and norms — not merely as custom or evidence but as a co-constitutive element of the common law itself.

**Caution:** In this particular case tikanga was not independently determinative of the outcome (the minority held it was irrelevant on these facts; the majority held it led to the same result as ordinary principles). The *NZ Law Association* analysis notes this was "not a suitable case for the court to make pronouncements of a general nature" and that because tikanga arose only in an interlocutory context without adversarial argument, the judgments' scope may be viewed with some caution by future courts ([NZ Law Association](https://www.thelawassociation.nz/incorporating-tikanga-what-ellis-will-be-remembered-for/)). The case resets parameters but does not itself decide a substantive dispute by tikanga principles.

### E.3.3 *Delgamuukw v British Columbia* [1997] 3 SCR 1010 — Canada

**Case:** *Delgamuukw v British Columbia*, [1997] 3 SCR 1010. Supreme Court of Canada. Decided 11 December 1997.

**Background:** Fifty-one hereditary chiefs of the Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en nations claimed Aboriginal title and jurisdiction over 58,000 square kilometres of northwest British Columbia. The trial judge had rejected their claims and refused to treat oral history as valid evidence.

**Outcome:** The Supreme Court of Canada unanimously ordered a new trial, holding that:
- The trial judge had *erred* in rejecting oral histories: "the laws of evidence must be adapted in order that this type of evidence can be accommodated and placed on an equal footing with the types of historical evidence that courts are familiar with."
- Aboriginal title is a *sui generis* right — a unique, hybrid interest not wholly explained by either common law or Aboriginal traditions alone — arising from prior occupation of land before Crown sovereignty.
- Aboriginal title encompasses the right to exclusive use and occupation of land for a wide variety of purposes, not merely traditional uses.

The decision also confirmed that the *content* of Aboriginal title was grounded in pre-existing Indigenous law, not merely in Crown recognition ([Wikipedia — Delgamuukw v British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia); [Library of Parliament Canada analysis](https://publications.gc.ca/Pilot/LoPBdP/BP/bp459-e.htm)).

**Relevance:** The requirement that oral testimony — the primary vehicle for transmitting customary legal knowledge in many Indigenous traditions — be placed "on an equal footing" with documentary evidence is a structural recognition that Indigenous legal epistemology must be accommodated by state courts.

### E.3.4 *Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia* [2014] SCC 44 — Canada

**Case:** *Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia*, 2014 SCC 44. Supreme Court of Canada. Decided 26 June 2014.

**Background:** The Tsilhqot'in Nation (a semi-nomadic First Nation) sought a declaration of Aboriginal title to approximately 1,750 sq km of their traditional territory in British Columbia, arising from an original 1983 dispute over commercial logging.

**Outcome:** The Supreme Court, led by McLachlin CJC, **unanimously** allowed the Tsilhqot'in appeal and granted a declaration of Aboriginal title — the *first successful Aboriginal title declaration* in Canadian legal history for a large area of non-treaty land. Key holdings:
- Aboriginal title requires occupation that is *sufficient, continuous, and exclusive* prior to Crown sovereignty.
- The Crown cannot authorise third-party uses of title land without the consent of title-holders or meeting a justification test.
- Governments and others "seeking to use the [titled] land *must obtain the consent of the Aboriginal title holders*."
- British Columbia had breached its duty to consult the Tsilhqot'in in its forestry licensing ([Wikipedia — Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsilhqot%CA%BCin_Nation_v_British_Columbia); [Tsilhqot'in National Government](https://tsilhqotin.ca/governance/tsilhqotin-rights-title/)).

**Relevance:** *Tsilhqot'in* is the most powerful precedent for the proposition that Indigenous law and custom — as it manifests in a community's territorial occupation and relationship with land — can ground enforceable property rights that bind the Crown itself.

### E.3.5 Brehon Law in Modern Courts: An Honest Assessment

**⚠ This requires careful framing.** There is **no** case in the modern Irish (post-1922) or United Kingdom legal system in which Brehon law has been successfully invoked as a binding or dispositive legal source. This is worth stating plainly to avoid over-claiming.

What *has* happened:

1. **The Dáil Courts (1920–1922):** A decree of the First Dáil in June 1920 establishing a parallel republican court system permitted Brehon laws to be *cited* in legal arguments, though with non-binding authority. In practice, little advantage was taken of this. One judge, James Creed Meredith, did apply Brehon law principles in a case involving recovery of expenses for a woman who had been sexually seduced and given birth — refusing to apply an English common law principle that men were not liable to support children born outside wedlock, and instead invoking the Brehon offence of *sleth*. He reported this was "uniformly applied in the Dáil Courts afterwards" ([History Ireland, Biggins, 2024](https://historyireland.com/afterlives-of-the-brehon-laws/); [thebrehonlawyer.com, 2021](https://thebrehonlawyer.com/2021/08/12/the-brehon-magistrate-enigma/)). The Dáil Courts were abolished in 1922.

2. **Irish fisheries litigation (1928–1948):** In a 1933 Supreme Court judgment concerning the River Erne, the majority (including Chief Justice Hugh Kennedy) accepted evidence about public fishing rights under the Brehon laws and found a private fishery company's title defective. This was subsequently diluted in later cases, and by 1948 the President of the High Court concluded that "the study of Irish law, seriously undertaken only in recent times, is still in its infancy and the conclusions of the most experienced scholars are tentative … the law texts cannot be used as a solid basis for any conclusion at all until they are edited and examined" ([History Ireland, Biggins, 2024](https://historyireland.com/afterlives-of-the-brehon-laws/)).

3. **Pre-1922 survivals:** The Brehon rules on succession were formally rejected by Irish courts in 1605 and 1607 (*Gavelkind* (1605) Dav. 49 and *Tanistry* (1607) Dav. 28), where the court held that native law failed the test of being "reasonable, certain, of immemorial usage and compatible with crown sovereignty" ([Royal Court of Breifne](https://royalcourtofbreifne.org/brehon-law/)). However, aspects of Brehon law could be raised in some property and customary-rights disputes through the 17th century.

**Conclusion for Brehon Consensus:** Brehon law cannot be cited as having modern binding legal status in any jurisdiction. The Dáil Courts episode and the fisheries litigation show it *can* be cited as historical evidence of customary legal principle, and that courts have occasionally been persuaded by such arguments in narrow factual contexts. But the system was formally extinguished and has not been revived as operative law. Its value for the project is as a *historical example* of a functional non-state enforcement architecture, not as a living legal source.

### E.4.1 Haudenosaunee Confederacy (Iroquois) — Great Law of Peace

**Description:** The Haudenosaunee ("People of the Longhouse") — comprising the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and (after c. 1722) Tuscarora nations — formed a Confederacy governed by the *Gayanashagowa* (Great Law of Peace), an oral constitutional framework requiring unanimous consensus of the Grand Council of fifty male chiefs (*hoyaneh*) for collective decisions, with matrilineal clan mothers holding the authority to nominate and remove chiefs.

**Dating and sources:** The Great Law's origin is traditionally placed around the 12th century CE (estimates range from c. 1142 to c. 1450); it was first written down in the 1880s. It remains the governing framework of Haudenosaunee communities today — the Grand Council is recognised as the longest-surviving democratic political institution in North America ([Wikipedia — Great Law of Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Law_of_Peace); [Smithsonian NMAI — Haudenosaunee Guide for Educators](https://americanindian.si.edu/sites/1/files/pdf/education/HaudenosauneeGuide.pdf)).

**Key features:** All Grand Council decisions required consensus. Clan mothers had absolute authority to nominate and remove chiefs. Lineage was matrilineal; women owned the land. The Onondaga served as "Firekeepers" (mediators) for deadlocked debates ([Smithsonian NMAI — Haudenosaunee Guide for Educators](https://americanindian.si.edu/sites/1/files/pdf/education/HaudenosauneeGuide.pdf); [Great Law of Peace text, Portland State University](https://web.pdx.edu/~caskeym/iroquois_web/html/greatlaw.html)).

**The contested "Iroquois Influence Thesis":** Claims that the Haudenosaunee Great Law directly inspired the US Constitution were advanced prominently by scholars Donald Grinde Jr. and Bruce Johansen from the 1970s onwards, and led to House Concurrent Resolution 331 (October 1988) acknowledging Haudenosaunee influence. However, this thesis is **substantially contested**:

- Haudenosaunee historian **Elisabeth Tooker** identified fundamental structural differences: decisions by consensus of male chiefs (not majority vote), representation by clan rather than population, and topics controlled by a single nation ([Wikipedia — Great Law of Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Law_of_Peace)).
- Stanford historian **Jack N. Rakove** found "lack of evidence in US constitutional debate records" for Iroquois influence, noting extensive European antecedents.
- University of Chicago law professor **Allison LaCroix** concluded: "On balance, the consensus appears to be that although British North Americans were certainly aware of the confederal nature of the Iroquois government, the case for causation has not been made" ([Liberty University thesis on Haudenosaunee and Constitution](https://digitalcommons.liberty.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2040&context=honors)).
- A weaker version of the thesis — that Iroquois governance was one of several influences on colonial thinking about federation — is more widely accepted.

**What can safely be claimed:** The Haudenosaunee Confederacy is a well-documented example of multi-generational, inter-nation consensus governance, operating without a centralised coercive state apparatus, with significant structural roles for women in political accountability. The specific claim that it shaped the US Constitution should be hedged with "contested" and the specific critics named.

**⚠ Caution:** The Confederacy was not "non-hierarchical" in any absolute sense. Decision-making was restricted to male chiefs (albeit selected and removable by women). The nations were not equal in weight. War was a significant feature of Confederacy history. Romanticising the Great Law as an Indigenous utopia misrepresents a complex political system.

### E.4.2 Athenian Democracy and Sortition

**Description:** Classical Athens (approximately 508–322 BCE) practised direct democracy including extensive use of *sortition* — selection by lot — for jurors, most administrative offices, and the Council of 500 (*Boulē*), producing a system in which ordinary citizens held real governance power without seeking election.

**Key features:** Any adult male citizen over 18 could speak and vote in the Assembly (*Ekklēsia*). Most magistracies were filled by lottery, not election, on the principle that selection by merit could be bought or manipulated. The *Boulē* (500 members, chosen annually by lot from each tribe) set the Assembly's agenda. Juries of up to 501 citizens, chosen by lot, decided legal cases with no professional judge ([Wikipedia — Athenian democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy)).

**⚠ Caution — the exclusions were structural, not incidental:**
- Women were absolutely excluded from all political participation ([Wikipedia — Athenian democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy)).
- Slaves were excluded — and constituted approximately 30–35% of the total population of Attica (estimates suggest c. 80,000 slaves in a total population of c. 240,000) ([hutsonk.weebly.com — population table, from Josiah Ober *Wealthy Hellas*](https://hutsonk.weebly.com/uploads/6/5/1/2/651297/athenian_democracy_sac_student_materials_0.pdf)).
- *Metics* (resident foreigners) were excluded, despite contributing significantly to the Athenian economy and serving as soldiers.
- Only approximately **10–20% of the total population** of Attica could actually participate ([Wikipedia — Athenian democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy)).

Athenian democracy was "rule of the people" only for a privileged minority of people. It was simultaneously *more* democratic (direct rather than representative) and *far less* democratic (excluding the majority) than modern democracy. The evidence that large-scale sortition-based governance *can* function is genuine; the evidence that it was egalitarian across the whole population is false.

### E.4.3 Quaker Consensus Decision-Making (Religious Society of Friends)

**Description:** Since the late 17th century, Quakers (Religious Society of Friends) have governed their affairs through a distinctive non-voting, consensus-seeking process called the "Quaker business method" or "sense of the meeting," operating without hierarchy, clergy, or chairpersons who outrank other participants.

**Origins and continuity:** The method emerged from George Fox's Gospel Order framework in the 1660s and has been practised continuously for over 350 years. It operates at every level of Quaker governance from local meetings to the national Yearly Meeting. There are approximately 7,000 local and area Meetings for Business annually in Britain alone ([Wikipedia — Quaker business method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaker_business_method); [Quaker Studies journal, cognitive science analysis, 2019](https://quakerstudies.openlibhums.org/article/id/15708/)).

**Key features:** No votes are ever taken. The clerk (a facilitator, not a decision-maker) identifies and articulates the "sense of the meeting" — the emergent collective judgement — which is then "minuted in the face of the meeting" (read aloud and corrected in real time). The process aims at unity rather than majority or unanimity; a single member may ask that a decision be deferred if they believe it has not been rightly reached. Decision-making authority rests formally with no individual — "nobody outranks anybody" ([ilym.org — Friends' Manner of Decision-Making](https://ilym.org/ilym/decision-making/); [thirdhaven.org analysis of Sheeran's principles](https://www.thirdhaven.org/decision_making.php)).

**⚠ Caution:** The Quaker business method is explicitly grounded in a specific theological belief — that there is "that of God in every one" — and its operational logic flows from this. It is not straightforwardly transferable to a secular or multi-faith civic context without adaptation. Furthermore, Quaker meetings are voluntary religious communities with shared theological commitments and social norms; whether the method scales to diverse, adversarial, or large-scale political contexts is unproven.

### E.4.4 Zapatista Autonomous Governance (EZLN / Chiapas, Mexico)

**Description:** Since 1994, the Zapatista National Liberation Army (EZLN) and the Indigenous communities of Chiapas, Mexico, have governed a substantial autonomous territory through rotating, non-salaried local councils, operating without Mexican government funding or authorisation, under the principle of *mandar obedeciendo* ("govern by obeying").

**Structure and scale:** In August 2003, the EZLN created the *Juntas de Buen Gobierno* (Good Government Councils), each based in one of the *caracoles* (administrative centres). By 2019, the structure had expanded to 12 *caracoles* and 43 bodies of self-government, covering Tseltal, Tojolabal, Mam, Tsotsil, Chol, and Zoque communities across Chiapas. The model operates at three levels: community, autonomous municipality, and Good Government Council ([Chiapas Support Committee, 19 years of the caracoles, 2022](https://chiapas-support.org/2022/08/18/to-govern-by-obeying-19-years-of-the-caracoles/); [EZLN announcement, 2019](https://chiapas-support.org/2019/08/18/ezln-creates-seven-new-caracols/)).

**Key features:** No position of authority is paid. Authority is conceived as collective service, not privilege. Council members rotate every 7–30 days (depending on zone), enabling broad participation over time. Only civilians may hold positions; the EZLN's military role is formally separate. Decisions are driven by participatory assemblies at community level; the Good Government Councils implement guidelines set at that level ([SIPAZ](https://www.sipaz.org/in-focus-the-path-of-the-caracol-towards-autonomy/?lang=en); [Raúl Zibechi essay on Zapatista autonomy, Schools for Chiapas PDF](https://schoolsforchiapas.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/Ra%C3%BAl-Zibechi-Zapatista-Autonomy-PDF.pdf)).

**⚠ Caution:** The Zapatista model operates in a context of ongoing conflict with the Mexican state and with paramilitary groups; its resilience owes partly to its militarised self-defence capacity. The territory remains officially within Mexico and its autonomy is de facto rather than legally recognised. The governance model has been analysed by sympathetic scholars and activist organisations; independent critical assessments are less common in the published literature. The question of whether the model would function without the cohesion provided by shared Indigenous identity, the shared history of the 1994 uprising, and the particular role of the EZLN as a unifying force, is unanswered.

### E.4.5 Rojava / Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (AANES) — Democratic Confederalism

**Description:** Since 2012, the Kurdish-led Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (AANES) — also known as Rojava — has implemented a governance model called "democratic confederalism" based on direct-democratic local assemblies, mandatory gender co-leadership (male and female co-chairs at every level), quotas for ethnic minorities, and ecology as a constitutional commitment.

**Ideological background:** Democratic confederalism was developed by imprisoned PKK leader Abdullah Öcalan, who had come into contact with the work of American anarchist and social ecologist **Murray Bookchin** through correspondence in 2004. Öcalan adopted Bookchin's framework of "libertarian municipalism" — a system of nested local assemblies replacing the nation-state — and adapted it for the Kurdish context, abandoning the previous PKK goal of a separate Kurdish state in favour of autonomous self-governance within existing states ([Wikipedia — Democratic confederalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_confederalism); [Frontiers in Political Science, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2021.815338/full); [Tandf — When Öcalan met Bookchin, 2018/2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14650045.2018.1508016)).

**Documented features:** All representational bodies have a minimum 40% female membership quota. Male and female co-chairs share leadership at every level. The legislative and executive councils are elected. Self-defence forces (YPG/YPJ) fall nominally under civilian democratic institutions. The system has achieved notable gender integration in governance structures and relative stability for a region in a civil war context ([Conciliation Resources / AANES analysis](https://www.c-r.org/accord/pioneering-peace-pathways/experiment-inclusion-informal-peacemaking-manbij-syria)).

**⚠ Caution — multiple serious concerns:**
1. **Political monopoly:** A critical external assessment by the Conciliation Resources found that AANES governance "has been criticised by other Kurdish and Arab political parties for failing to extend power to communities not connected to the PYD." Contrary to rhetoric about multi-ethnic participation, "key positions are held by Kurds despite the Administration's emphasis on inclusion, equality, and ethnoreligious diversity," and "the reality of instituting political and cultural diversity in practice has created tensions and representational challenges" ([Conciliation Resources](https://www.c-r.org/accord/pioneering-peace-pathways/experiment-inclusion-informal-peacemaking-manbij-syria)).
2. **Geopolitical dependency:** The AANES's survival has depended on US military support against ISIS and has involved complex alliances that raise questions about true autonomy ([Frontiers in Political Science, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2021.815338/full)).
3. **Existential crisis:** As of early 2026, the AANES faces an existential threat. Following the fall of the Assad regime in late 2024, the new Syrian transitional government in Damascus launched military operations against AANES territory. A ceasefire was agreed in January 2026 but its durability is uncertain. A March 2025 agreement called for the integration of AANES military and civilian institutions into the Syrian state, which the Kurdish movement is resisting by continuing to advocate for a "democratic, decentralised Syrian state" at the April 2025 Kurdish Unity Conference in Qamishli ([Truthout, February 2026](https://truthout.org/articles/rojavas-experiment-in-revolutionary-autonomy-is-facing-its-greatest-threat-yet/)). The project's survival as an autonomous experiment is in serious doubt at the time of writing.
4. **Top-down imposition concern:** Some analysts note that democratic confederalism was in some areas perceived to have been implemented "top-down" by the PYD, creating frictions with communities not organically aligned with its ideological framework ([Conciliation Resources](https://www.c-r.org/accord/pioneering-peace-pathways/experiment-inclusion-informal-peacemaking-manbij-syria)).

## E.5 Synthesis: Using These Examples Responsibly

The purpose of Section E is to refute the claim that non-hierarchical, consent-based, rule-based governance is a utopian fantasy with no historical precedent — the "there is no alternative" position. The examples above accomplish this purpose. However, the following synthetic cautions apply to the section as a whole:

1. **None of these examples is fully non-hierarchical.** Brehon law had rigid rank-stratification; the Haudenosaunee restricted formal political roles to male chiefs (albeit selected and removable by women); Athenian democracy excluded the majority; Quaker governance is hierarchical in the sense that weight is given to those with "experience" and "the Light"; Zapatista governance rests on the EZLN's military authority; Rojava on a single party's ideological framework.

2. **Each example is context-specific.** Small-scale kinship communities (Brehon *túatha*; Haudenosaunee clans) operated under constraints of shared identity and mutual visibility that may not translate to large, diverse, anonymous modern polities. The burden of proof lies with those who would scale these models, not with those who question scaling.

3. **Romanticisation corrupts the argument.** The strongest case for the *possibility* of alternatives is made by accurate, sober accounts that acknowledge defects. An over-claimed account is easily discredited; an honest one is not.

4. **The Graeber/Wengrow framework provides useful vocabulary** — the three primordial freedoms; the distinction between seasonal and permanent hierarchy; the concept of "getting stuck" — but must be used with acknowledgement of the methodological critiques by Morris, Feinman, Smith, Appiah, and Bell.

5. **The legal cases (Mabo, Ellis, Delgamuukw, Tsilhqot'in) demonstrate something narrow but important:** state courts of liberal democracies have formally recognised that Indigenous and customary legal systems did not simply cease to exist upon colonisation. This is a powerful point about legal pluralism and the ongoing vitality of non-state law. It does not establish that those systems were non-hierarchical or consent-based in all their features.

## F.1 What Positioning and Branding Research Says About Naming a Genuinely New Category

### F.1.1 Category Design: *Play Bigger* (Ramadan, Peterson, Lochhead & Maney, 2016)

The foundational text for category-creation strategy is *Play Bigger: How Pirates, Dreamers, and Innovators Create and Dominate Markets* (HarperBusiness, 2016), co-authored by Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, and Kevin Maney. The book, described on its [Google Books page](https://books.google.com/books/about/Play_Bigger.html?id=vNuUCgAAQBAJ) as revealing "a groundbreaking discipline called category design," establishes several key propositions directly relevant to Brehon Consensus.

**The winner-takes-all economics of category creation.** The authors' proprietary research — examining roughly 26,000 US-headquartered venture-backed technology companies founded since 2000 — found that Category Kings (the dominant company in a new category) typically capture approximately 76 per cent of the total market capitalisation of their space, leaving all competitors fighting over the remaining 24 per cent. As the [Play Bigger Time-to-Market-Cap report](https://www.playbigger.com/time-to-market-cap-report) states: "Most Category Kings win 76 percent of the category's valuation." This is not market *share* — it is market *cap*, a far more durable and compound advantage. The implication for non-commercial projects is analogous in reputational and adoption terms: the organisation that names and frames a new civic category becomes its de facto standard-setter.

**Category design defined.** The authors define the discipline as "the process and mindset of aiming to build a category king" ([blas.com summary](https://blas.com/play-bigger/)). More precisely, in the words of Christopher Lochhead in a [Marketing Journal interview](https://www.marketingjournal.org/play-bigger-an-interview-with-christopher-lochhead/): "Categories are the organizing principle for customers for centuries. Customers use categories as mental 'tags' for new products and companies. These tags make it easier for people to understand new products and offers."

**The category name.** The *Play Bigger* playbook is explicit that a category name must do specific cognitive work: "A consumer category name has to be concise and descriptive — social network, ride-sharing" ([blas.com summary](https://blas.com/play-bigger/)). Crucially, "the category name should speak to the problem being solved and eventually become a line item on people's budgets (attention or monetary)." The broader marketing journal interview elaborates: "The category name is comprised of the market type, the nature of the problem, and the audience who has the problem. It's a name that will evolve over time as your category expands."

**Languaging.** *Play Bigger* introduced the term "languaging" to describe the practice of inventing specific vocabulary that gives a category shape and texture. As [Dave Bailey's blog summarising the book](https://www.dave-bailey.com/blog/how-to-create-a-new-startup-category) explains: "Category creators create unique names for the features a product needs to have to enter the category. This practice is called 'languaging' — a term that is, itself, a perfect example of inventing language to support a new category." Examples cited in the book include Salesforce popularising "SaaS," Uber turning "ride" into a verb, and Slack replacing "internal IM" with "team chat." In each case, the language was not describing the product — it was conditioning the market to accept a new category container. From the [pulserevops.com summary of *Play Bigger*](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs0174): "When Salesforce popularized 'SaaS,' when Uber turned 'ride' into a verb, when Slack replaced 'internal IM' with 'team chat,' they were not naming products."

**The category vs. the product distinction.** *Play Bigger* insists that companies do not win products — they win categories. The category winner is not necessarily the best product but the one that most effectively frames the *problem*. As Bailey summarises: "Don't compete — reframe the problem and redefine the category. Name the outcome, not the product." This is directly applicable to Brehon Consensus: rather than describing what the technology *does*, the naming strategy should describe the *problem state it solves* and the *world after* it exists.

**Lightning strikes and conditioning.** Category creation requires "conditioning" — repeated public messaging that names the problem, proposes the new solution shape, and crowns its solver. A category is not made in a single product launch; it requires sustained, coordinated framing. The *Play Bigger* framework calls the peak event a "Lightning Strike": a 3–6-month coordinated moment after a Point of View is approved, where "all pieces of category design and POV [must be] written down" and disseminated.

### F.1.2 Positioning: *The Battle for Your Mind* — Al Ries & Jack Trout (1981)

Ries and Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981) remains the most widely cited framework in marketing for why names and categories matter at the cognitive level. The core thesis: "Positioning is not what you do to a product. Positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect" ([pulserevops.com summary](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs307); [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/8786/1b7389455b54528d31ab94a96d2bfa47096b.pdf)).

**The mind as the battlefield.** Ries and Trout argue that in an "overcommunicated society," the human mind is overloaded and resistant to change. It organises knowledge in ranked "ladders" — one ladder per category — and resists restructuring once a position is established. "The easiest way to get into a person's mind is to be first" ([Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/8786/1b7389455b54528d31ab94a96d2bfa47096b.pdf)). History shows that the first brand into a category typically gets twice the long-term market share of the second brand.

**The risk of borrowing a wrong-category word.** Ries and Trout are explicit about the dangers of attaching an existing category's name to something genuinely new. When a word already has an established ladder in the mind, a new entrant using that word inherits the entire set of existing expectations, assumptions, and limitations of the existing category. They advise: "You have to select names that are almost, but not quite, generic" ([valchanova.me summary](https://valchanova.me/positioning-ries-trout-book-summary/)). A wrong-category word forces the audience to fight two cognitive battles at once: understanding what the new thing is, and unlearning what the borrowed word used to mean.

**Being first creates lasting impressions.** "Being first creates this incredibly powerful lasting impression. Dramatically. History shows the first brand in a category tends to capture like double the long-term market share of the number two brand" ([YouTube summary, Business Book Club](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y_ZUF2kHCZM)). For Brehon Consensus, which may be among the first serious civic-tech projects to attempt structured peer consensus at community scale, there is a genuine first-mover naming opportunity.

**The name is the hook.** "The name is the hook that hangs the brand on the product ladder in the prospect's mind" ([businessbookclub.substack.com](https://businessbookclub.substack.com/p/free-book-summary-positioning-by)). A weak or generic name handicaps everything. Ries and Trout advocate for names that: (a) are distinct; (b) are easy to remember; (c) hint at what the product does; and (d) can be legally and cognitively owned.

**Creating a new ladder.** For products that do not fit existing categories, Ries and Trout offer the same counsel as *Play Bigger*, decades earlier: "If you can't be first in a category, create a new category you can be first in" ([pulserevops.com summary](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs307)). The tactic is to identify an existing ladder in the mind that is adjacent, and reposition from it — demonstrating what the new thing is *not*, while naming what it *is*.

### F.1.3 Academic Marketing Research: Category Labels, Typicality, and Consumer Comprehension

**Prototype theory (Eleanor Rosch, 1971–1978).** The dominant cognitive science framework for how humans categorise new things is prototype theory, developed primarily by psychologist Eleanor Rosch at UC Berkeley. As described on [Wikipedia's Prototype Theory article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype_theory), the theory emerged in 1971 and "has been described as a 'Copernican Revolution' in the theory of categorization." Rather than defining categories by necessary and sufficient conditions (the classical Aristotelian view), prototype theory holds that categories are organised around *prototypical* exemplars — the most typical member — and that membership is a matter of degree, not binary inclusion or exclusion.

Rosch's 1975 experiments asked subjects to rate items on a scale of 1 to 7 for typicality within a category: for furniture, a *couch* is rated very typical; a *wardrobe*, less so. The cognitive implication is that when someone encounters a new word for a new thing, they will immediately try to map it to the nearest existing prototype they hold — and will judge the new thing against those expectations. A [Design Research Society paper on categorisation](https://dl.designresearchsociety.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2605&context=drs-conference-papers) summarises: "categories are formed around their most representative instances, which have something like a prototypical character."

For Brehon Consensus, this means: whatever category noun is chosen, audiences will immediately retrieve their mental prototype for that noun and evaluate whether Brehon "counts" as a good instance of it. If the word chosen maps to a prototype the product does not match well, audiences will either reject the categorisation or form incorrect expectations.

**The first-label dominance effect.** A rigorous experimental study in the *Journal of Consumer Research* — ["What Is It?" — Categorisation Flexibility and Consumers](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/27/4/489/1810433) (Moreau, Markman & Lehmann, 2001) — demonstrated that "the first plausible category label provided to the consumer significantly influences their categorisations, expectations, and preferences." The study showed that "when two plausible category labels are activated sequentially, the label presented first has a dominant influence on consumers' perceptions of a new product." Crucially: "category labels override feature similarity as a factor predicting the type of inferences made about missing information." Even three-year-old children preferred to base inferences about a new object on its stated category membership rather than its appearance.

This finding has profound implications for naming: the first category noun applied to Brehon Consensus will tend to stick and will shape all subsequent public understanding, regardless of how carefully the product itself is designed. There is no neutral labelling strategy.

**Labels shape comprehension, even when forgotten.** A follow-up study, ["The Explanatory Effect of a Label"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8770803/pdf/fpsyg-12-745586.pdf) (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2022), demonstrated that "the label effect persists over time" and that "the labeled category retains the influence of the label even when the label itself is forgotten." Participants in the label condition "remembered more information from the vignettes but tended to forget an artificial label; however, they used other words from the disorder domain instead." In other words, a category noun imports a *semantic schema* that persists after the specific word fades.

**Category labelling strategy: marketing research evidence.** A [Yale School of Management blog post on category naming](https://som.yale.edu/blog/defining-category-using-behavioral-science-to-further-product-naming-conventions) synthesises relevant behavioural science: "Most consumer decisions are processed using System 1 processing. When a consumer encounters a product in the marketplace, they need to be able to very quickly and intuitively determine some basic characteristics of the product and how it might be used." The post recommends testing names on two axes simultaneously: *understanding* (does the name communicate what this is?) and *appeal* (does it create positive associations?). It also warns: "Words that combine the familiar with the original tend to stick" — an empirical finding that supports the portmanteau and borrowed-but-repurposed strategies documented in Section F.2 below.

An [ISARDER study on new product category labels](https://isarder.org/index.php/isarder/article/view/1221) found that "when faced with a new product, consumers categorise the product in the direction of the label provided in the advertisement and away from other more likely categorisations" — and that "consumers who are uncertain about cider's product category showed lower attitude towards the product and purchase intention scores than consumers who are more confident about their category decisions." **Uncertainty about what something is = lower trust and adoption.** The label must resolve ambiguity, not create it.

## F.2 How Now-Familiar New Categories Got Their Nouns

The following etymologies reveal consistent patterns: category-defining nouns are almost always short, concrete, borrowed from ordinary language (often physical or mechanical) but *repurposed* to fit a new digital or social context, and they frequently work by analogy. They rarely describe the technology; they describe the *activity* or the *experience*.

---

### F.2.1 "Search Engine" (attested from 1988)

The compound noun "search engine" entered the vocabulary as a descriptor for programs that found information in computer databases. According to [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/search): "Search engine attested from 1988." The individual components are thoroughly familiar English: "search" (from Old French *cerchier*, Latin *circare*, to wander/traverse) and "engine" (a machine, a mechanism). The pairing was immediately comprehensible because it described a tool that *performs* a search — an engine *for* searching. It imports the connotation of mechanical power and automaticity, without being technically precise about how the searching happens.

The term became dominant as the early web grew: [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/search-engine) and [Wikipedia's search engine article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_engine) both trace the first practical search engines to 1990 (Archie, by Alan Emtage at McGill University), and the term stabilised as the category populated in the mid-1990s with Yahoo!, WebCrawler, AltaVista, and others. Notably, the tools initially called themselves "search engines" even before there was a stable category — the noun preceded full comprehension of the category.

**Lesson:** A compound of two very familiar words, describing the *function* (search) through a mechanical metaphor (engine). No technical jargon required; the analogy (an engine that does work) did the cognitive labour.

---

### F.2.2 "Social Network / Social Networking" (coined 1954; internet application 2003+)

The term "social network" was coined in 1954 by Australian sociologist John Arundel Barnes in his paper "Class and Committees in a Norwegian Island Parish" (*Human Relations*, VII, 1954), as confirmed by [historyofinformation.com](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=715) and [Wikipedia's Social Network article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_network). Barnes used it to describe a social structure of nodes (individuals or organisations) connected by ties of varying strength — a sociological metaphor for the invisible web of human relationships.

The digital application came fifty years later. As the [eps415hre.weebly.com explainer](https://eps415hre.weebly.com/social-networking-personal-and-professional.html) notes: "Danah Boyd and Nicole Ellison used the term 'Social network site' to describe the new phenomenon of interacting by computers. After careful consideration, the term 'Network' was changed to 'Networking' to emphasize relationship initiation." Boyd & Ellison's 2007 paper formally defined "social networking site" and the phrase entered popular usage through Friendster (2002), MySpace (2003), and Facebook (2004).

**Lesson:** A term borrowed wholesale from academic sociology and applied almost unchanged to a new digital context. The analogy was apt enough that no translation was needed — human social ties had always existed; the platform was just a new substrate for them. The word "network" imports a reassuring metaphor (connections, infrastructure) that describes both the technical architecture and the human experience.

---

### F.2.3 "Podcast" (coined February 2004)

"Podcast" is perhaps the most instructive coinage for Brehon Consensus because it was invented almost by accident, under deadline pressure, by a journalist — not by the technologists building the medium.

The coinage is thoroughly documented. Ben Hammersley, a British journalist writing for *The Guardian*, was filing an article on "downloadable audio content automatically retrieved via RSS feed." The article ran 12 February 2004. Hammersley has described the coinage in video testimony quoted on [stultus.in](https://stultus.in/notes/podcast-etymology/):

> "I submitted the article on time. But then I got a phone call from my editor about 15 minutes before the presses were due to roll saying, 'Hey, that piece is about a sentence short for the shape of the page.' And so I just made up a sentence which says something like, 'But what do we call this phenomenon?' And then I made up some silly words."

Those words, as recorded in the article and confirmed by [Wikipedia's Podcast article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast) and [SmarterMSP](https://smartermsp.com/pioneers-in-tech/), were: "Audioblogging? **Podcasting?** GuerillaMedia?" The word "podcast" is a portmanteau of "iPod" (Apple's market-dominant portable media player in 2004) and "broadcast" (itself a repurposed agricultural term — "to sow seeds broadly"). As [The Allusionist transcript](https://www.theallusionist.org/transcripts/podcast-podcast) notes: "Portmanteauing is a very common way of creating new words, because it works: people recognise the components of the existing words, which helps them feel familiar with the new word."

The term was not immediately adopted. It lay dormant for six months until developer Danny Gregoire introduced it to the iPodder-dev mailing list in September 2004, from where Adam Curry popularised it. By December 2005, the New Oxford American Dictionary declared "podcast" word of the year.

**Lesson:** Short portmanteau of two familiar words. The first component (iPod) anchored it to a specific device — a limitation Hammersley later regretted, since the content can be accessed on any device. The second component (broadcast) imports connotations of editorial scheduling and public address. The word worked because it described the *experience* (listening to audio you had chosen, at a time of your choosing, like radio but different) rather than the technology. Its near-accidental origin suggests that the right noun does not have to be the product of systematic design: it has to *ring true* when heard.

---

### F.2.4 "Wiki" (coined 1995 by Ward Cunningham)

Ward Cunningham launched WikiWikiWeb on 25 March 1995 on c2.com, described by him as "the simplest online database that could possibly work." The etymology is directly documented in Cunningham's own correspondence, published at [c2.com/doc/etymology.html](https://c2.com/doc/etymology.html) and confirmed by [Wikipedia's WikiWikiWeb article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikiwikiweb):

> "Wiki wiki is the first Hawai'ian term I learned on my first visit to the islands. The airport counter agent directed me to take the wiki wiki bus between terminals. I said what? He explained that wiki wiki meant quick."

"Wiki wiki" is a reduplication of the Hawaiian word *wiki* meaning "quick" — in Hawaiian, doubling a word intensifies it. Cunningham chose the word deliberately: "I thought 'wiki wiki web' was more fun to say than 'quick web'." He also noted a formal resonance: his software used unusual doublings as formatting clues (double carriage-return for paragraph; double single-quote for italic; double capitalised-word for hyperlink), so the doubled name mirrored the doubled syntax.

Crucially, as a [Medium article on wiki etymology](https://medium.com/technology-and-language/why-wiki-c3e100d04070) observes, Cunningham "was not trying to duplicate any existing medium, like mail, so he didn't want a name like electronic mail (email) for his work." He explicitly chose a word *from outside the existing vocabulary* of computing — a Hawaiian word that no English speaker would map to any prior technology — precisely to signal that this was something genuinely new. The word "wiki" meant nothing in English except through its coiner's explanation; yet its sound and the story of its origin made it memorable and unique.

**Lesson:** A borrowed foreign-language word, chosen for sound and story rather than description. Its unfamiliarity in English was a feature, not a bug — it created no false expectations. The origin story (Hawaiian airport shuttle) is memorable and humanising, aiding adoption. Wikipedia (2001) then cemented the category noun by combining it with Greek *paedia* (education) to create "quick education."

---

### F.2.5 "Blog / Weblog" (coined December 1997 and May 1999)

The etymology of "blog" is a two-stage coinage. First, Jorn Barger coined "weblog" on 17 December 1997 for his *Robot Wisdom* site. As the [CLRN explainer](https://www.clrn.org/where-does-the-word-blog-come-from/) documents: "'Weblog' is a direct shortening of 'web' and 'log' — borrowed from the nautical tradition of maintaining a ship's log, a detailed record of events, observations, and data related to a voyage."

Second, in April or May 1999, programmer Peter Merholz *jokingly split* the word "weblog" into the phrase "we blog" in the sidebar of his personal site peterme.com — as documented by [Wikipedia's Blog article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weblog). This was interpreted as a short form of the noun, and Evan Williams at Pyra Labs subsequently used "blog" as both noun and verb, devising the term "blogger" in connection with their Blogger product. The Oxford English Dictionary added "blog" in 2002 or 2003.

**Lesson:** "Weblog" was a transparent compound of "web" (medium) + "log" (form/format); "blog" was an accidental abbreviation that became dominant because of its terseness. The word "log" imports connotations of chronological recording, personal testimony, and expedition — resonances that proved apt for the medium. The shortening to "blog" was not planned; it succeeded because short words propagate faster in informal language. The nautical origin of "log" (a piece of wood thrown overboard to measure a ship's speed) was entirely forgotten — what persisted was the "running record" connotation.

---

### F.2.6 "Spreadsheet" (pre-digital term repurposed, c.1979)

"Spreadsheet" is the clearest example of a pre-existing physical-world noun successfully repurposed for a digital category. As [Wikipedia's Spreadsheet article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet) records: "Since at least 1906 the term 'spread sheet' has been used in accounting to mean a grid of columns and rows in a ledger." The physical spreadsheet was a large sheet of paper that *spread* across facing pages of a bound ledger, with expenditure categories along the top and invoice items down the left margin. It was the accountant's physical tool.

Dan Bricklin, co-creator of VisiCalc (1979), explicitly stated: "We did not invent the word 'spreadsheet.' The name 'electronic spreadsheet' was applied to VisiCalc precisely because it was like the paper spreadsheets" ([bricklin.com](http://www.bricklin.com/firstspreadsheetquestion.htm)). VisiCalc went on sale 24 October 1979 and became the first "killer application" for personal computers. The name required no explanation to its target audience of business users: they already knew what a spreadsheet was and what it was for. The word "electronic" was merely the adjective that marked the difference — which could then be dropped once electronic spreadsheets became the default.

As [community.cadence.com notes](https://community.cadence.com/cadence_blogs_8/b/breakfast-bytes/posts/visicalc): "The term 'spreadsheet' was already in existence and referred to large pads of paper marked out in rows and columns to make doing the same sorts of calculations by hand."

**Lesson:** The most elegant category noun strategy — borrow a word the audience already uses for the analogue predecessor, strip the qualifiers (paper, physical), and the digital version inherits all prior understanding while the word evolves to mean the new thing exclusively. Requires an actual analogue predecessor that the audience knows.

### F.2.7 Synthesis: Lessons from Category-Noun Etymology

The six cases above yield a short, high-confidence set of principles:

| Case | Mechanism | Length | Origin domain | Analogy imported |
|------|-----------|--------|---------------|-----------------|
| Search engine | Functional compound | 2 words | General English | Mechanical power |
| Social network | Academic term repurposed | 2 words | Sociology | Physical relationships |
| Podcast | Portmanteau | 1 word | Brand + agriculture | Broadcast radio |
| Wiki | Foreign borrowing | 1 word | Hawaiian language | Speed, simplicity |
| Blog | Abbreviation of compound | 1 word | Nautical/digital | Personal record-keeping |
| Spreadsheet | Physical predecessor repurposed | 1 word | Accounting | Columnar data entry |

**Key lessons:**
1. **Short wins.** All successful category nouns are one or two words. None are phrases or explanations. If the name requires a clause to explain itself, it is not yet a name.
2. **Borrowed-but-repurposed everyday words are cognitively safe.** They import existing semantic schema (reassuring) while the new context redefines them (accurate). Pure neologisms require the audience to build a schema from scratch — higher cognitive load, lower adoption.
3. **Analogy-friendly.** Every successful name makes the new thing feel like a natural member of a familiar family — even when the technology is genuinely unprecedented. "Wiki" felt like nothing before it, yet the Hawaiian meaning (quick) made the behaviour (quick editing) immediately intuitive once explained.
4. **The coinage does not need to be the work of experts.** "Podcast" was a deadline filler. "Blog" was a joke. The rightness of a name is discovered through adoption, not designed through committee.
5. **Physical-world metaphors persist.** "Log," "engine," "network," "spread" — all physical, all pre-digital. Physical metaphors anchor abstract digital behaviour to embodied human experience.

## F.3 Evidence on Which Words Convert Versus Alienate Non-Technical Civic Audiences

### F.3.1 "Governance" — Research Finding: Technocratic, Abstract, and a Liability

The word "governance" presents the most significant risk for Brehon Consensus, given that the project sits in civic-tech space and will need to recruit participation from non-specialist community members.

**FrameWorks Institute evidence.** The FrameWorks Institute is the most authoritative non-partisan body conducting empirical research on how US public audiences understand civic and institutional language. Beginning in 2004, in partnership with Demos and the Council for Excellence in Government (CEG), FrameWorks undertook a multi-year investigation into public understanding of government-related language. Their 2008 summary guide [*How to Talk About Government*](https://www.frameworksinstitute.org/app/uploads/2020/03/How_To_Talk_government.pdf) makes the following evidence-based statements:

> "1. The word 'government' poses an obstacle to productive thinking. The word 'government' is so freighted with pejorative baggage that it should be used with caution and is best used only after other terms that establish its public mission. Without this redirection, government is universally greeted with derision."

And in their 2006 Message Memo [*How to Talk About Government*](https://www.frameworksinstitute.org/app/uploads/2020/03/Government_Message_Memo-1.pdf):

> "The word 'government' is so freighted with pejorative baggage that it should be used with caution and is best used only after other terms that establish its public mission. Without this redirection, government is universally greeted with derision — deep-seated ridicule, learned and conditioned over time."

Their core "Do/Don't" guidance is direct: "DON'T: Invoke the word 'government' at the start of any communications; you will cue up the 'joke' response and likely the equation of government with electoral politics."

⚠ **Note on "governance" vs "government":** The FrameWorks research above refers specifically to the word "government." The closely related term "governance" is not explicitly tested in the FrameWorks Government research (the Brehon project team should verify directly with FrameWorks). However, the same semantic field is clearly implicated: "governance" carries all the formal institutional connotations of "government" — structure, authority, rules, decisions — with the additional academic abstraction of being a nominalization (a verb turned into a noun, one step removed from action). In linguistics, nominalizations typically test as more abstract and less emotionally engaging than the corresponding verb forms. The word "govern" implies agency; "governance" implies system.

**PACE Civic Language Perceptions Project evidence.** In 2019, Philanthropy for Active Civic Engagement (PACE), in partnership with Topos Partnership and Wake Forest University, conducted 28 small-group focus groups and a nationally representative survey of 1,006 respondents, testing public perceptions of approximately 20 terms central to civic engagement work. The full results are published in the [PACE Civic Language Perceptions Project report](https://www.pacefunders.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/PACE-Language-Perception-Project_May-16.pdf).

The top-level finding is unambiguous:

> "One theme came through clearly: Americans simply do not think or talk in the terms our field uses to describe democracy and civic engagement. The words and phrases practitioners use to describe this work — like civic engagement, activism, civility, and advocacy — do not tend to appear in the everyday vernacular of people beyond the scope of our field."

The report's Topos Partnership qualitative analysis, published separately ([Topos Civic Engagement report](https://www.pacefunders.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Topos-Civic-Engagement-report-29Apr2019.pdf)), frames the central challenge: "It seems Americans have no strong, clear sense of what a healthy, civically-engaged democracy or society entails. This appears to be an important reason why they have so little shared vocabulary in this domain."

Critically: "Not only do the vast majority of Americans not relate to the language most commonly used within our field to describe civic engagement and democracy, many of them also reported that it felt 'like somebody else's language.'" This finding applies directly to "governance" — a word used extensively by civic-tech practitioners, policy professionals, and academic researchers, but rarely by community members describing what they actually want or do.

The PACE survey's quantitative data (graph ratings on a 1–5 scale) shows that terms like "civic engagement," "civic health," and "civil society" — all more accessible than "governance" — scored lower on likeability and usage frequency than simpler, warmer terms like "common ground," "justice," and "democracy" itself. "Governance" was not directly tested in the 2019 PACE study; the 2022 expanded PACE language survey of 5,000 voters across 21 terms is available at [PACE's Language Register page](https://www.pacefunders.org/language-register/), though ⚠ the specific term-by-term breakdown for "governance" could not be verified in the publicly available summary materials at time of research.

**The self-organising / governance axis.** "Self-organising" is a term from complexity theory and biology that occasionally appears in civic-tech design discussions. There is no A/B test data specifically comparing "governance" versus "self-organising" for civic audiences; ⚠ the comparison below is based on inferred principles from adjacent research rather than direct empirical testing.

"Self-organising" has the advantage of implying agency and emergent structure without imposing a formal institutional frame. However, it risks feeling either too technical (imported from systems theory) or, to some audiences, too anarchic or abstract — it does not communicate *how* the self-organisation works. "Governance" at least communicates that there are rules and decisions; "self-organising" communicates only that participation is distributed. Neither term communicates the experience of being involved.

---

### F.3.2 "Moderation" — Digital Familiarity, Limitations for Civic Context

"Moderation" entered mainstream digital vocabulary through online forums, social media platforms, and comment sections. In this context it has a specific and increasingly fraught meaning: the removal or restriction of content by a platform or its designated agents. A paper at [SSRN on digital content moderation](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/SSRN_ID4911354_code6921270.pdf?abstractid=4911354&mirid=1&type=2) describes it as "communal content moderation" and notes that even user-based moderation models are understood primarily through the lens of content removal and enforcement.

As the R Street Institute piece ["Community and Content Moderation in the Digital Public Hypersquare"](https://www.rstreet.org/commentary/community-and-content-moderation-in-the-digital-public-hypersquare/) observes: "The very term content 'moderation' is increasingly misleading, given that it is commonly understood to mean removing 'bad' things instead of selecting good things." The article argues for shifting to the term "content mediation" instead, precisely because moderation has become too narrowly associated with policing.

For Brehon Consensus, "moderation" imports this content-removal baggage. It also implies a moderator — a third-party authority — and may therefore communicate top-down imposition rather than peer participation. A moderator "moderates" behaviour; the word does not describe a consensus process. Its Latin root (*moderari*, to measure, keep within bounds) is helpful etymologically but is not how ordinary people understand the word.

Additionally, "moderation" in common English means "doing things in moderation" — avoiding extremes — which is a second, entirely different semantic pathway that creates ambiguity. Does "Brehon moderation" mean content policing, or avoiding extremes of opinion, or something altogether different?

---

### F.3.3 "Mediation" — Professional Register, Conflict Connotations

"Mediation" has a specific professional register: it describes a structured process in which a neutral third party helps two or more parties in *dispute* reach a resolution. It is used primarily in legal, labour, and family law contexts. For civic audiences, the word "mediation" will most readily activate a schema of *conflict resolution between adversaries*.

For Brehon Consensus — which is presumably not positioned as a conflict-resolution service between parties in dispute, but as a positive consensus-building process — this connotation is partly misleading. Mediation suggests that the parties could not agree on their own and needed outside help; it implies a pre-existing disagreement, not a shared project of forming collective judgement.

That said, mediation has one significant advantage over "governance" and "moderation": it implies a *process* (something happens, a mediator works) and a *relational* quality (between people), rather than an abstract system or a policing function. It is less institutional-feeling than governance and less punitive-feeling than moderation.

⚠ No A/B test data or direct civic-audience research specifically comparing "moderation" versus "mediation" as civic-engagement framings was found during this research.

---

### F.3.4 "Decision-Making" — Functional, but Procedural

"Decision-making" is a transparent compound that communicates clearly what happens: a decision gets made. It is procedural rather than institutional, which is an advantage over "governance." It also implies collective action (decisions are usually made by groups in civic contexts) without prescribing the mechanism.

The limitation is its length (three syllables), its corporate-boardroom register (decision-making is the language of management), and its focus on *output* (a decision) rather than the experience of *participation*. It describes the goal — reaching a decision — without describing the process of getting there or the quality of deliberation involved.

For non-technical civic audiences, "decision-making process" is accessible. But as a category noun for a product or protocol, it is too descriptive of function and too little evocative of character. "Spreadsheet" and "social network" succeeded partly because they named experiences, not procedures.

---

### F.3.5 "Jury" — High Recognition, Civic Resonance, But Double-Edged

"Jury" is one of the most potentially useful words for a civic-consensus product. Its properties:

- **High cultural familiarity.** Every adult in the English-speaking world knows what a jury is. The concept of "a jury of one's peers" is both legally foundational and culturally resonant.
- **Procedural legitimacy built in.** A jury implies fairness, deliberation, impartiality, and binding decision — all properties desirable for Brehon Consensus. Research at CHI 2020, ["A Civics-Oriented Approach to Platform Governance" (Digital Juries)](http://digitaljuries.com/juries/Digital_Juries__CHI2020.pdf), found that "digital juries are perceived as more procedurally just than existing common platform moderation practices" and that "users preferred a deliberative jury over a blind-voting jury." A follow-up study in [*Interacting with Computers* (2025)](https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article/37/4/234/7745684) confirmed: "both liberal and conservative moderators thought jury moderation was fair, valued jurors' individual voices, and achieved satisfactory outcomes."
- **Civic connotations.** A jury is not a bureaucratic instrument — it is a civic institution where ordinary people exercise collective authority. This resonance aligns well with Brehon's civic ambitions.

**Limitations and risks:**

- A jury reaches a *verdict* (from Latin *veredictum*, truly said) — it adjudicates between adversarial positions. For contexts where the goal is collaborative deliberation rather than adversarial judgement, "jury" may import inappropriate framing. The [SSRN paper on digital jurors](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/SSRN_ID4911354_code6921270.pdf?abstractid=4911354&mirid=1&type=2) notes that the digital jury model "is designed to resemble the legal jury trial system" but observes that "its democratic legitimacy is limited due to the omission of important procedural elements such as deliberation."
- Juries are randomly selected and time-limited; they produce verdicts, not ongoing governance. If Brehon is an ongoing structure rather than an ad hoc panel, "jury" may mislead on duration and process.
- In some cultural contexts, "jury" carries adversarial and punitive associations that could alienate participants who see themselves as community collaborators rather than judges.

---

### F.3.6 Summary: Word Comparison for Brehon Consensus Naming

| Word | Accessibility | Institutional feeling | Civic connotation | Process clarity | Risk |
|------|--------------|----------------------|-------------------|-----------------|------|
| Governance | Low (abstract/insider) | Very high | Neutral-to-negative | Low | Over-formalises; alienates non-specialists |
| Moderation | Medium (digital-familiar) | Medium | Negative (punitive) | Low | Content-policing baggage |
| Mediation | Low-medium (professional) | Medium | Mixed (implies conflict) | Medium | Conflict-resolution frame may mislead |
| Decision-making | High (transparent) | Medium-high | Neutral | High | Procedural/corporate; no character |
| Self-organising | Low (technical) | Low | Positive-neutral | Very low | Too abstract; no mechanism visible |
| Jury | Very high (cultural familiarity) | Low-medium | Strongly positive | High | Adversarial/verdictive; time-limited frame |

The evidence suggests:

1. **Avoid "governance" as a primary noun** for non-specialist audiences. FrameWorks Institute research confirms its cousin "government" triggers derision and institutional alienation reflexively. "Governance" inherits this baggage with added academic abstraction.

2. **"Jury" is the most promising borrowed civic noun**, offering high familiarity, built-in procedural legitimacy, and strong civic resonance. The risks (adversarial framing, verdict rather than deliberation) can be mitigated by compound naming (e.g., "community jury," "deliberative jury") or by careful framing that emphasises the *deliberation* phase rather than the verdict.

3. **"Moderation" should be avoided** unless the product is specifically positioned as content policing, which Brehon is not. The word is already damaged in civic-tech contexts.

4. **A purpose-coined portmanteau or borrowed-but-repurposed everyday word** — along the lines of "wiki," "podcast," or "blog" — may ultimately be the right path. The word "council" (a gathering of people who advise or deliberate) and "assembly" (a gathering called together for a purpose) were not tested directly in the research above but deserve exploration: both are pre-democratic in origin, widely understood, and free of the technocratic register of "governance." "Forum" (Latin *forum*, public space for assembly and commerce) is similarly accessible and civic.

5. **The word that serves Brehon Consensus best will be short, rooted in physical-world or civic experience, describe the activity/experience rather than the mechanism, and generate a story** — as "wiki" did (Hawaiian airport shuttle) and "podcast" did (a journalist's last-minute padding). The right noun is not the one that is most accurate; it is the one that is most immediately *felt* as true.

# Section G — Synthesis: Rules, Positioning, Risks, and Reading List

> **What this section is.** Sections A–F gathered the evidence. This section converts it into decisions. It contains four things: (1) the **top ten evidence-based rules** for the leaflet and website, each tied to its strongest supporting source; (2) **three positioning angles**, each with a sample one-liner, ranked for fit with non-technical grassroots organisers; (3) the **biggest messaging risks** with evidence-based mitigations; and (4) a short **annotated reading list**. British English throughout. Where the underlying evidence is contested or could not be verified, the ⚠ flag is carried through from the source section.

---

## G.1 The Top Ten Evidence-Based Rules

These are ranked by leverage — how much they will improve comprehension and trust per unit of effort — not by the order they appear in the brief.

### Rule 1 — Lead with the power contrast, not the mechanism.

Open with *who holds power and how that changes*, before any explanation of juries, federation, or vouching. Every successful comparable project does this: Mastodon's "[Social networking that's not for sale](https://joinmastodon.org/)", Nostr's "controlled by users, not platforms", platform.coop's "[stakeholders before shareholders](https://platform.coop/)" (Section D, Pattern 1). The framing literature agrees: lead with values, not policy detail ([FrameWorks Strategic Frame Analysis](https://www.frameworksinstitute.org/); Lakoff, with the caveat in Section B that his *prescriptions* are better-supported than his neural claims ⚠). For Brehon, the contrast is: *your community makes and keeps its own rules — instead of a platform's rules being applied to you.*

### Rule 2 — Name the pain before you name the solution.

Loomio opens by naming the problem the reader already feels — "[Lost in email. Too fast in chat. Endless in meetings.](https://www.loomio.com/)" — before proposing anything (Section D, Pattern 4). This is also the most robust finding behind narrative persuasion: a concrete, recognised situation engages attention and emotion in a way that an abstract benefit statement cannot ([Green & Brock, narrative transportation, 2000](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2000-16701-016); Section B2). For Brehon, the recognised pain is the experience of bad moderation: *being banned with no explanation, no appeal, and no way back — or watching it happen to someone else.*

### Rule 3 — Explain the unfamiliar through one concrete, everyday analogy.

Analogies work by mapping the relational structure of something known onto something new ([Gentner's structure-mapping](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/Gentner83.pdf); Section A1). Gick & Holyoak showed that giving people a worked analogue raised solution rates on a hard problem from ~30% to ~76% ([Gick & Holyoak, 1980/1983](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0010028580900134); Section A1.2). The Fediverse's telephone analogy ("you can ring anyone regardless of their provider") is the canonical civic-tech example (Section D, Pattern 2). For Brehon, the strongest candidate is the **community jury**: a familiar institution that already carries the ideas of randomly-chosen peers, due process, hearing both sides, and a reasoned decision. Use one analogy well rather than three loosely.

### Rule 4 — Use concrete verbs, plain words, and short sentences. Cut the jargon.

Plain language is not dumbing-down; it measurably improves comprehension, speed and reader trust. Oppenheimer showed that needless complexity *lowers* perceived intelligence of the author ([Oppenheimer, 2006, "Consequences of Erudite Vernacular"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.1178)); Shulman et al. found jargon reduces trust, engagement and interest in the subject ([Shulman et al., 2020](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1041794X.2020.1788677)); Morkes & Nielsen found concise, scannable web copy improved measured usability by ~124% ([Morkes & Nielsen, 1997](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)). Section D's Pattern 3 confirms comparable projects favour concrete verbs ("raise, manage and disburse money") over abstract nouns. Concretely: prefer "decide together" to "governance", "repair the harm" to "restorative sanction mechanism".

### Rule 5 — Avoid "governance" as the headline noun.

FrameWorks research finds the cognate "government" triggers reflexive derision and institutional alienation; "governance" inherits that baggage and adds academic abstraction, being a nominalisation one step removed from action (Section F.3.1). ⚠ Note the documented caveat: FrameWorks tested "government", not "governance" specifically, so this is a strong inference rather than a direct test, and the project should consider commissioning targeted message-testing. The naming evidence points instead toward short, physically- or civically-rooted words that describe the *experience* — "jury", "council", "assembly", "forum" — following how "wiki", "podcast" and "blog" became categories by borrowing the familiar and repurposing it ([*Play Bigger*](https://www.playbigger.com/); Section F.1, F.2). "Jury" scores highest on familiarity and built-in procedural legitimacy, with the manageable risk that it can sound adversarial or verdict-focused (Section F.3.5–F.3.6).

### Rule 6 — Frame reputation as *earned standing for helping govern*, never as a score.

This is the single highest-risk concept in the project and must be framed deliberately. The danger is the "social-credit / Black Mirror *Nosedive*" misread, which is a live cultural reference for exactly this audience ([Section C3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nosedive); note the China Social Credit System is itself widely misunderstood, [Xu, 2023](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly)). The evidence-based fix is to anchor standing in *contribution to governance* (serving as a juror, helping resolve disputes) and to state explicitly what does **not** count — posting, liking, sharing, popularity ([indirect-reciprocity research, Nowak & Sigmund, 2005](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature04131); Section C3 contribution-vs-engagement distinction). Say the negative out loud: *"This is not a popularity score. You cannot farm it by posting."*

### Rule 7 — Earn trust by showing your working and admitting limits.

For a sceptical, technically-literate audience, calibrated honesty out-performs polish. The Hovland model separates expertise from *trustworthiness*, and trustworthiness is what a new project most needs ([Hovland & Weiss, 1951](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1953-00177-001); Section C1). The pratfall effect shows that admitting a flaw can *increase* likeability for an otherwise-competent source ([Aronson, Willerman & Floyd, 1966](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1966-12498-001); Section C1). Transparency-as-trust ("show your work") and visible governance documents are low-cost, high-credibility signals ([Linux Foundation OSS trust guidance](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/hubfs/Reports/improving_trust_security_in_oss_projects.pdf); Section C1). Practically: state clearly that this is early-stage software, name what it cannot yet do, and link to the public reasoning behind decisions.

### Rule 8 — Pre-empt the crypto misread before the reader raises it.

Civic-tech and FOSS audiences carry strong, structural hostility to anything that pattern-matches to crypto — see the Mozilla backlash ("[planet-incinerating Ponzi grifters](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/mozilla/mozilla-and-web3/)", 2022), the Wikimedia community's 71% vote to stop accepting crypto donations (2022), and Mastodon's explicit distancing (Section C4, Cases 2–5). Because Brehon involves "reputation" and "decentralisation" — both crypto-adjacent on the surface — silence will be read as evasion. State plainly and early: *no blockchain, no token, no coin, nothing to buy or sell.* This is the documented pre-emptive-disassociation strategy in Section C4.

### Rule 9 — Define accountability as *active repair*, and evoke the cancel-culture experience without using the label.

"Cancel culture" is a tribal, culture-war term: Pew found Americans split sharply along partisan lines on whether it is "accountability" or "censorship" ([Pew, 2021, n≈10,093](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2021/05/19/americans-and-cancel-culture-where-some-see-calls-for-accountability-others-see-censorship/)), and Fahey et al. found partisanship overrides framing ([Fahey, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/19401612241228595)) — so *use the experience, not the label* (Section C2.1). The restorative-justice frame supplies the alternative: redefine accountability as demanding *active repair* rather than passive punishment ([Zehr, *Changing Lenses*](https://www.heraldpress.com/9780836135121/changing-lenses/); OJJDP's "support without accountability… accountability without support" formulation; Section C2.2). Loretta Ross's "calling-in" gives the warm register: structured, consensual accountability with a route back ([Ross, TED 2021 / *Calling In*, 2025](https://lorettajross.com/); Section C2.1).

### Rule 10 — Make fair *process* the promise, and end on agency and hope.

Tom Tyler's procedural-justice programme shows people accept even unfavourable decisions when the process is fair — voice, neutrality, respect, trustworthiness — and that perceived legitimacy predicts cooperation far more than outcomes or risk of punishment ([Tyler, *Why People Obey the Law*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691126739/why-people-obey-the-law); Section C2.3). This is Brehon's core promise: *a fair hearing, by your peers, with reasons given, and a way back.* Pair it with hope and efficacy rather than fear: the activism literature finds that agency-and-efficacy framing mobilises better than threat ([Bandura on collective efficacy](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2000-13991-001); [Marshall Ganz, Public Narrative](https://leadingchangenetwork.org/); Section B4), and gain-framing slightly out-performs loss-framing on average, though the effect is small (⚠ [O'Keefe & Jensen meta-analyses, r≈.03](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17934940/); Section B4.2). Close on what the community *gains the power to do*, not on what it fears.

## G.2 Three Positioning Angles (Ranked)

Each angle is internally consistent and evidence-backed; they differ in which truth they lead with. They are ranked for fit with the stated audience — non-technical grassroots organisers (mutual aid, co-ops, unions, activist groups) — and the stated tone (plain, warm, sober; not startup hype, not manifesto, not Celtic romance).

### Angle 1 (Recommended) — "Accountability without annihilation": fair process for communities that govern themselves

**Sample one-liner:** *"When something goes wrong in your community, Brehon Consensus gives you a fair way to handle it — a jury of your own members, clear reasons, real repair, and a way back — instead of a silent ban from a platform that was never yours."*

**Why it ranks first.** It leads with the recognised pain (Rule 2) and the power contrast (Rule 1) at once; it foregrounds *fair process*, which is the most robustly evidenced lever in the whole brief ([Tyler](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691126739/why-people-obey-the-law); Section C2.3); and it sidesteps both the "governance" word-trap (Rule 5) and the cancel-culture label-trap (Rule 9) while still evoking the experience. It matches the audience's existing values — due process, solidarity, no one disposable — without ideological jargon. The phrase "accountability without annihilation" is the project's own and tests well against the restorative-justice evidence base (Section C2.2). This is the warmest, soberest of the three.

### Angle 2 — "Rules you wrote, kept by people you trust": community self-determination

**Sample one-liner:** *"Brehon Consensus lets your community write its own rules and keep them through juries of randomly-chosen members — so the people affected by a decision are the people who make it."*

**Why it ranks second.** It leads with consent and self-determination, which is the empirically validated formula for durable self-governing institutions ([Ostrom's design principles 3 and 5](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/governing-the-commons/A8BB63BC4A1433A50A3FB92EDBBB97D5); Section C2.4). It resonates strongly with co-ops and unions, whose entire culture is member self-rule, and echoes platform.coop's "stakeholders before shareholders" (Section D). It ranks below Angle 1 only because "rules" and "self-governance" are slightly more abstract than a remembered injustice, and because it does less to defuse the moderation/reputation fears up front. Excellent as the *second beat* after Angle 1's opening.

### Angle 3 — "An old idea for a new problem": peer justice, brought online

**Sample one-liner:** *"Long before platforms, communities settled disputes through their own people and their own customs. Brehon Consensus brings that idea — peer juries, repair over punishment, a fair hearing — to the online communities you build today."*

**Why it ranks third (use with care).** The historical-legitimacy anchor is a real and well-used pattern (Section D, Pattern 7), and the historical evidence that consent-based, restorative governance has genuinely existed is solid enough to rebut "this has never worked" ([Brehon law features](https://celt.ucc.ie/); customary-law cases like *Mabo* and *Ellis v R*; Section E). **But** this angle carries the highest risk of tipping into Celtic-heritage romance, which the brief is explicitly told to avoid, and the honest historical record is mixed (Brehon law also embedded slavery, rigid hierarchy and unequal status for women — Section E.2; no modern court has applied Brehon law as binding — Section E.3.5 ⚠). Lead with this *only* if it is framed as "one example among many" and immediately grounded in the present-day mechanism. Strongest as a single supporting paragraph or an "origins" footnote, **not** as the headline.

## G.3 The Biggest Messaging Risks (and Evidence-Based Mitigations)

### Risk A — The reputation-as-surveillance / social-credit misread.

**The risk.** "Reputation" + "online community" pattern-matches instantly to social-credit scoring and *Black Mirror: Nosedive* — and this audience is primed to recoil from it (Section C3). Get this wrong and the project reads as the opposite of what it is.

**Mitigation (evidence-based).** Anchor standing in *contribution to governance* and state the negatives explicitly: it is not a popularity score, not earned by posting/liking/sharing, not a number that follows you around, and the cost of a violation is set *by your own community*, not by the software ([indirect-reciprocity research](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature04131); contribution-vs-engagement distinction, Section C3). Consider avoiding the bare word "reputation" in headline copy in favour of "standing earned by helping" or simply "trust your community places in you" (Rule 6). Name the *Nosedive* fear and dismiss it directly rather than hoping the reader does not think of it.

### Risk B — The cancel-culture / culture-war trap.

**The risk.** Any project touching online accountability can be sucked into the cancel-culture flame war, where partisanship overrides argument and you lose half the room before you have spoken ([Pew, 2021](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2021/05/19/americans-and-cancel-culture-where-some-see-calls-for-accountability-others-see-censorship/); [Fahey, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/19401612241228595); Section C2.1).

**Mitigation.** Do not use the term "cancel culture" at all — not even to oppose it (using it activates the frame, per Section B and Section C2.1). Instead *evoke the experience* everyone recognises (unilateral exclusion, no due process, no route back) and offer the restorative alternative in plain language: repair, a hearing, reintegration. Use Loretta Ross's warm "calling-in" register rather than any "anti-cancel" register ([Ross](https://lorettajross.com/); Section C2.1). Pre-empt the "soft on crime / soft on abusers" rebuttal with the active-accountability framing: repair is *more* demanding than a ban, not less ([Zehr](https://www.heraldpress.com/9780836135121/changing-lenses/); OJJDP; Section C2.2).

### Risk C — The crypto / blockchain misassociation.

**The risk.** "Decentralised" + "federated" + "reputation" reads as crypto to a FOSS/civic audience whose hostility is structural, not incidental — and that hostility is well-documented and severe (Mozilla, Wikimedia, Mastodon, SourceHut; Section C4).

**Mitigation.** Pre-emptive disassociation (Rule 8): say in plain words, early and unmissably, that there is no blockchain, no token, no coin, and nothing to buy, sell or speculate on. Lead the "decentralised/federated" explanation with the *familiar* federation analogy (email, the telephone network, Mastodon) rather than anything that sounds like a ledger (Section C4; Section D Pattern 2). Do not bury the disclaimer in an FAQ — the audience will look for it immediately.

### Risk D — Over-claiming at the alpha stage.

**The risk.** Hype creates its own failure mode through expectation disconfirmation: promise a finished revolution, ship early software, and the gap itself destroys trust ([Persuasion Knowledge Model, Friestad & Wright, 1994](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/21/1/1/1815159); hype-and-trust research, Section C2). Savvy audiences also actively discount marketing-speak.

**Mitigation.** Calibrated honesty and the pratfall effect (Rule 7): state plainly that this is early-stage and experimental, name what it does *not* yet do, and invite scrutiny. Replace superlatives with specifics. "Show your working" — link to the public reasoning, the open code, the governance docs ([Linux Foundation guidance](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/hubfs/Reports/improving_trust_security_in_oss_projects.pdf); Section C1). Under-promise; let the mechanism speak. A sober tone is also exactly what the brief specifies.

### Risk E — Celtic-heritage romance.

**The risk.** The name invites a misty, nationalist, "noble ancient Irish wisdom" register that would undercut the project's credibility with a critical audience and misrepresent the actual history (Brehon law also embedded slavery, hierarchy and unequal status for women; no modern court has applied it as binding — Section E.2, E.3.5 ⚠). The Graeber & Wengrow material that supports "other ways have existed" is itself genuinely contested by named scholars (Section E.1).

**Mitigation.** Treat Brehon law as *one example among many*, cited accurately and soberly, never as a blueprint or a heritage claim (Section E.5). Keep the historical material light, present-tense-focused, and honest about the bad parts where relevant. Lead with the mechanism (Angles 1–2), not the origin story (Angle 3). If the name's etymology is explained at all, explain it once, plainly, and move on. The test from Section E: invoking these examples is legitimate *only* to rebut "this has never existed" — over-claiming their egalitarianism undermines that very purpose.

## G.4 Annotated Reading List

A short, prioritised list for the person writing the leaflet and website. Starred (★) items are the highest-leverage starting points.

### Books

- ★ **Chip Heath & Dan Heath, *Made to Stick* (2007).** The single most practical book for this task: concreteness, simplicity, stories, and the "curse of knowledge" — all directly applicable to the leaflet. (Section A, Section B2.4.)
- ★ **Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014).** The clearest treatment of the curse of knowledge and how to write plainly for readers who do not share your context. (Section A2.3.)
- **Howard Zehr, *Changing Lenses* (1990/2005).** The foundational restorative-justice text; the source of the "accountability as active repair" framing that underpins Angle 1. (Section C2.2.)
- **Loretta J. Ross, *Calling In: How to Start Making Change with Those You'd Rather Cancel* (2025).** The warm, practical register for accountability-without-annihilation; pairs with her TED talk. (Section C2.1.)
- **Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990).** The eight design principles for durable self-governing institutions — especially consent to rules (P3) and graduated, initially-forgiving sanctions (P5). The empirical backbone of Angle 2. (Section C2.4.)
- **Al Ries & Jack Trout, *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981)** and **Ramadan, Peterson, Lochhead & Maney, *Play Bigger* (2016).** Read together for the naming/category decision: the risk of the wrong category word, and how genuinely new categories get their nouns. (Section F.1.)
- ⚠ **David Graeber & David Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything* (2021).** Powerful for the "other ways have existed" argument — but read the named critiques alongside it (Morris, Feinman, Scheidel and others) before citing; it is genuinely contested. (Section E.1.)

### Papers and reports

- ★ **Tom R. Tyler, *Why People Obey the Law* (1990; 2006 ed.)** and the procedural-justice programme. The strongest single evidence base in the brief: fair process predicts legitimacy and cooperation far more than outcomes. (Section C2.3.)
- **Daniel M. Oppenheimer (2006), "Consequences of Erudite Vernacular Utilised Irrespective of Necessity."** The experimental case for plain language — complexity lowers perceived intelligence. (Section A3.3.)
- **Shulman et al. (2020) on jargon and trust**; **Morkes & Nielsen (1997) on web readability.** Two compact empirical anchors for plain, scannable copy. (Section A3.)
- **Green & Brock (2000), narrative transportation.** Why a concrete story out-persuades a bullet list. (Section B2.2.)
- ⚠ **O'Keefe & Jensen gain/loss-framing meta-analyses (2006–2009).** Important corrective: the gain-vs-loss effect is real but small (r≈.03) — do not over-rely on framing tricks. (Section B4.2.)
- **Pew Research Center (2021), "Americans and Cancel Culture."** The data behind avoiding the term entirely. (Section C2.1.)

### Toolkits and practitioner resources

- ★ **FrameWorks Institute — Strategic Frame Analysis™ and the "Culture, Mindsets" guides** ([frameworksinstitute.org](https://www.frameworksinstitute.org/)). The most directly usable framing toolkit; note the ⚠ caveat that "governance" specifically was not tested. (Section B1.3, Section F.3.1.)
- **Common Cause Foundation — values and framing guidance** ([valuesandframes resources](https://commoncausefoundation.org/)). Intrinsic-vs-extrinsic values framing for activist audiences. (Section B1.4.)
- **Marshall Ganz / Leading Change Network — Public Narrative ("Story of Self, Us, Now")** ([leadingchangenetwork.org](https://leadingchangenetwork.org/)). The organising-narrative structure for the "Now / call to action" beat. (Section B4.5.)
- **Linux Foundation — "Improving Trust and Security in Open Source Projects."** Concrete, low-cost trust signals (SECURITY.md, transparent governance docs) for a small open-source project. (Section C1.)
- **Nielsen Norman Group — web-writing and progressive-disclosure articles** ([nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/)). For structuring the website: chunking, layering, the inverted pyramid. (Section A4.)
